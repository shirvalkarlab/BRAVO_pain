# Issue 2: Does DBS relief "wash in" with time on a setting, and can we model that without modelling calendar time?

> Research report for RCS08 (bilateral Medtronic Percept RC, chronic pain)
> All citations below were verified against PubMed/NCBI E-utilities (abstract text pulled directly); PMID and DOI given for each. Nothing here was invented; anything I could not verify is listed separately under "Unverified" at the end.

---

## Bottom line

1. **The general phenomenon the PI describes — a stimulation benefit that builds in over time on an unchanged setting, sometimes over minutes, sometimes over weeks — is well established across neuromodulation, but the *timescale* is target- and symptom-specific, and no study has measured it directly for the sensing/stimulation pair BRAVO uses (L 1-3+/R 0-3+, ACC/OFC region unconfirmed).** Parkinson's tremor washes out in seconds-minutes, bradykinesia/rigidity in tens of minutes, axial signs in hours (Temperli et al. 2003); dystonia and depression benefit continues to build for **weeks to months** after a setting is fixed (Kupsch et al. 2006; Ruge et al. 2011; Mayberg et al. 2005); pain-specific human data on this exact question are thin.

2. **There is a well-described, medication-side analogue that is a close structural match to what the PI is describing: the "long-duration response" (LDR) to levodopa, a benefit with a half-life of *days*, riding on top of a short-duration response with a half-life of *minutes to hours*** (Nutt & Holford 1996). This is a useful template because it names exactly two things BRAVO's current model conflates into one number ("current in force"): a fast component the clinic ladder can see, and a slow component only the ambulatory (REDCap) stream can see.

3. **A cumulative-dose or "leaky-integrator" model of stimulation history is a *current* effect, not a *time* effect, and is fully consistent with the PI's standing rule that time is modelled nowhere.** It is a weighted average of the device's own delivered current, contains no calendar-time term, and its zero-memory limit (time constant = 0) is mathematically identical to the model already in use ("current in force"). This means it can be tested by asking the existing purged, block-of-time cross-validation (decision 240 in the project's own log) to pick a time constant, with "the best time constant is 0" as the null result that would say wash-in adds nothing beyond what is already modelled.

4. **RCS08's own three-block finding at an unchanged setting (1.51 → 0.53 → −0.06) is exactly the *shape* a wash-in effect would produce, but with only one held setting it cannot be told apart from a pure calendar-time trend, a placebo/expectation effect, or a co-occurring change in another treatment** — the same collinearity problem the PI has already ruled must be handled by attributing drift to current, not time. Breaking this collinearity requires data where current goes up **and back down**, which the ladder's own down-leg rows (already recorded, per decision 213) can supply cheaply.

5. **Direct electrophysiological (LFP) evidence for a *slow* (hours-to-days) wash-in is essentially absent; the LFP changes that have been measured after a stimulation change are either very fast (seconds to about a minute — evoked resonant activity, beta suppression) or very slow and hard to disentangle from disease progression or the post-implant "stun effect" (months, in one chronic-sensing case series).** If RCS08's pain relief really does take hours to days to build in, Percept's own chronic band-power log is, in principle, the right instrument to look for a matching slow signal — but nobody has shown this signal exists in a stimulation (as opposed to a medication or disease-progression) context, and a fast closed-loop controller sensing on a seconds-to-minutes timescale would have no way to see it even if it did.

## Evidence by theme

### A. Time courses of DBS effects, onset and offset, by target and symptom

**Parkinson's disease, subthalamic nucleus (STN) — the best-characterized case, and the one that shows the phenomenon is not one number but several.**

- **Temperli P, Ghika J, Villemure JG, Burkhard PR, Bogousslavsky J, Vingerhoets FJ. "How do parkinsonian signs return after discontinuation of subthalamic DBS?" *Neurology*. 2003;60(1):78-81. PMID: 12525722. DOI: 10.1212/wnl.60.1.78.**
  35 patients, STN DBS, studied 5-240 minutes after stimulation was switched off. Tremor worsened within minutes; bradykinesia and rigidity worsened more slowly over half an hour to an hour; axial signs (posture, gait) worsened slowest, over 3-4 hours, with 90% of the total motor worsening reached only after 2 hours. Turning stimulation back on reversed the pattern, faster than the washout, especially for axial signs. **Why it matters for RCS08:** this is the cleanest demonstration in any DBS literature that "the same lead, the same disease, four different signs" can have four different time constants — a caution against assuming one wash-in time constant will describe every one of RCS08's watched bands or every pain dimension (NRS, VAS, McGill). Limitation: motor signs in PD, not pain; washout only (no data on a slower *build-in* over days).

- **Nutt JG, Holford NH. "The response to levodopa in Parkinson's disease: imposing pharmacological law and order." *Ann Neurol*. 1996;39(5):561-73. PMID: 8619540. DOI: 10.1002/ana.410390504.**
  Not DBS — this is the classic pharmacological description of the levodopa response, but it is the closest published structural analogue to what the PI is describing. The paper separates a **short-duration response** (half-life of minutes to hours, tracks plasma drug level) from a **long-duration response (LDR)** (half-life of **days**), and shows that small changes in effective drug level can produce large changes in the size of the response over time. **Why it matters:** it is the field's template for exactly the two-timescale structure the PI is proposing for stimulation ("how long has this current been on" as a slow variable riding under the instantaneous relationship). Limitation: pharmacological, not electrical, mechanism; whether DBS wash-in shares the LDR's specific days-scale half-life is not established — the analogy is structural, not quantitative.

**Dystonia, globus pallidus internus (GPi) — the strongest published case of a clinical benefit building in over weeks to months.**

- **Kupsch A, Benecke R, Müller J, et al. (Deep-Brain Stimulation for Dystonia Study Group). "Pallidal deep-brain stimulation in primary generalized or segmental dystonia." *N Engl J Med*. 2006;355(19):1978-90. PMID: 17093249. DOI: 10.1056/NEJMoa063618.**
  40 patients, randomized sham-controlled trial, GPi DBS. At 3 months, blinded dystonia severity score improved by 15.8 points with active stimulation versus 1.4 with sham (p<0.001); after 6 months of open-label stimulation the sham group caught up to a similar benefit once switched on. **Why it matters:** it shows the benefit is still substantial and still similar in shape at 3 and 6 months, i.e., a genuinely slow-building effect measured with a placebo control (ruling out simple regression-to-the-mean) — the single best RCT-level demonstration that "give it months" is sometimes the right clinical instruction, not an excuse. Limitation: dystonia is a movement disorder with a different presumed circuit mechanism than pain; n=40 is per-arm modest by RCT standards.

- **Ruge D, Tisch S, Hariz MI, et al. "Deep brain stimulation effects in dystonia: time course of electrophysiological changes in early treatment." *Mov Disord*. 2011;26(10):1913-21. PMID: 21547950. DOI: 10.1002/mds.23731.**
  Longitudinal TMS study tracking cortical inhibition and synaptic plasticity markers at 1, 3, and 6 months after GPi DBS. Cortical inhibition normalized gradually over months, on the same time course as clinical benefit; a plasticity marker changed non-monotonically, dropping immediately after surgery before rising toward normal. **Why it matters for RCS08:** direct (if indirect-measure) evidence that the slow clinical wash-in in dystonia has a matching slow *physiological* signature — i.e., it is not simply patients or raters becoming more favorable over time, there is a brain-side correlate. This is the kind of proof-of-concept the PI would need on the pain side (Section B) and does not yet exist there. Limitation: cortical TMS measures, not local LFP; small sample typical of DBS physiology studies.

**Depression, subcallosal cingulate (SCC/area 25) — the psychiatric case, again weeks-to-months, and again requiring careful attribution.**

- **Mayberg HS, Lozano AM, Voon V, et al. "Deep brain stimulation for treatment-resistant depression." *Neuron*. 2005;45(5):651-60. PMID: 15748841. DOI: 10.1016/j.neuron.2005.02.014.**
  6 patients, open-label, chronic SCC DBS. 4 of 6 had "striking and sustained remission" with reduced local blood flow and downstream limbic/cortical PET changes. The original report and the wider literature it launched describe a differential timeline: some behavioral change is visible with intraoperative test stimulation, but the durable antidepressant effect develops over weeks to months of continuous stimulation. **Why it matters:** this is the field's founding demonstration that "on" is not "working yet" for a limbic-circuit target, and that the delay is not an artifact of one bad study — it has been replicated across a decade of SCC-DBS follow-up work by the same and other groups. Limitation: open-label, n=6, no sham arm in the original report (later randomized trials, not detailed here, had a more mixed record); depression outcome measures, not pain.

**OCD, ventral capsule/ventral striatum — response can take months, with wide individual variation.**

- **Alonso P, Cuadras D, Gabriëls L, et al. "Deep Brain Stimulation for Obsessive-Compulsive Disorder: A Meta-Analysis of Treatment Outcome and Predictors of Response." *PLoS One*. 2015;10(7):e0133591. PMID: 26208305. DOI: 10.1371/journal.pone.0133591.**
  Meta-analysis, 31 studies, 116 subjects. Pooled Y-BOCS (symptom severity) reduction 45.1%, pooled responder rate 60.0%. **Why it matters:** OCD DBS response is documented in the wider literature (case series, not itself independently verified here beyond this meta-analysis) to typically take weeks to several months to reach its full size, with individual patients classed informally as "rapid" versus "slow" responders — again supporting a slow, variable wash-in rather than a fixed clinic-visit-scale effect. Limitation: this meta-analysis reports the pooled endpoint effect size, not the time-course data itself; the rapid-versus-slow-responder framing comes from the broader OCD-DBS literature and is stated here as context, not as an independently verified quantitative claim.

**Pain DBS specifically — the literature most relevant to RCS08, and also the thinnest and most equivocal.**

- **Lempka SF, Malone DA Jr, Hu B, et al. "Randomized clinical trial of deep brain stimulation for poststroke pain." *Ann Neurol*. 2017;81(5):653-63. PMID: 28380672. DOI: 10.1002/ana.24927.**
  10 patients with post-stroke pain, bilateral ventral striatum/anterior limb of the internal capsule (VS/ALIC) DBS — an affective-circuit target, not a classical sensory one. Design: 1 month after implant, randomized to active-vs-sham for 3 months, then crossover for another 3 months (blinded phase 6 months total), followed by an **18-month open-label phase**. The trial was **negative** on its primary endpoint (≥50% Pain Disability Index improvement in ≥50% of patients) but found significant improvement on affective-pain measures (depression, affective McGill subscale). **Why this matters critically for the PI's question:** the blinded crossover used **no washout period** between the 3-month active and 3-month sham blocks. If VS/ALIC analgesia genuinely takes weeks-to-months to build in (as the depression/OCD literature on the same target suggests it might, since VS/ALIC is a limbic-affective target) and comparably long to wash out, then a patient randomized active-then-sham could have been carrying real, lingering benefit into the "sham" arm — biasing the trial toward a *null* primary result even if VS/ALIC pain relief is real. This is a plausible, testable alternative explanation for one of the few controlled pain-DBS trials that exist, and it is a direct illustration of why washout design matters for exactly the question the PI is asking. Limitation: n=10, exploratory; the paper's own authors do not raise this washout critique in these terms — this is an inference from the design as reported, not a stated finding.

- **Boccard SG, Pereira EA, Moir L, Aziz TZ, Green AL. "Long-term outcomes of deep brain stimulation for neuropathic pain." *Neurosurgery*. 2013;72(2):221-30. PMID: 23149975. DOI: 10.1227/NEU.0b013e31827b97d6.**
  85 patients, single center, 12-year experience, sensory thalamus and/or periventricular gray (PVG) targets. ~70% retained implants at 6 months; 66% had benefit; effectiveness varied by diagnosis (89% in amputees, 70% in stroke). Improvements exceeding 30% on pain/quality-of-life scales persisted past 42 months, with some measures **still improving beyond the first year**. **Why it matters:** the largest published pain-DBS case series with a stated long tail of continuing improvement — consistent with, though not proof of, a genuinely slow-accruing benefit rather than a fixed effect reached quickly. Limitation: uncontrolled case series, no sham arm, mixes multiple pain etiologies and targets; "continuing improvement" in an open series cannot rule out selection (patients doing poorly drop out) or placebo drift.

- **Young RF, Chambi VI. "Pain relief by electrical stimulation of the periaqueductal and periventricular gray matter. Evidence for a non-opioid mechanism." *J Neurosurg*. 1987;66(3):364-71. PMID: 3493333. DOI: 10.3171/jns.1987.66.3.0364.**
  45 patients, PAG/PVG stimulation followed ≥1 year. 16 (35.6%) developed **tolerance** — progressively *less* effective relief from an unchanged setting. No cross-tolerance to morphine; naloxone reversed relief no more than placebo did, arguing against a purely opioid mechanism. **Why it matters:** tolerance is the mirror image of wash-in — a *negative* dose-history effect, where the same current becomes less effective the longer/more it has been used, rather than more. Any dose-history model built for RCS08 must be able to represent both signs (a term that can help or hurt), not assume relief only ever grows with exposure. Limitation: 1987 methodology (no modern imaging, informal statistics by today's standard); sensory-thalamus/PVG targets, not confirmed to match RCS08's implant location.

- **Sims-Williams H, Matthews JC, Talbot PS, et al. "Deep brain stimulation of the periaqueductal gray releases endogenous opioids in humans." *Neuroimage*. 2017;146:833-42. PMID: 27554530. DOI: 10.1016/j.neuroimage.2016.08.038.**
  5 patients with deafferentation pain, PAG DBS, [11C]diprenorphine PET before/during stimulation. Stimulation reduced opioid-receptor tracer binding by 15.5% in caudal dorsal PAG (opioid release), but naloxone did not block the analgesia, and the amount of opioid release did not correlate with the amount of pain relief. **Why it matters:** establishes a plausible *slow* biochemical mechanism (receptor occupancy, downstream signaling) that could in principle build in or saturate over minutes-to-hours of stimulation, distinct from a fast synaptic/circuit mechanism — but the paper's own data show this particular opioid signal doesn't explain the analgesia's *size*, so it cannot yet be used to justify a specific wash-in time constant. Limitation: n=5, single PET session per condition (cannot resolve a within-session time course), target is PAG/PVG not confirmed for RCS08.

**Motor cortex stimulation (MCS) — a genuinely delayed pain-relief mechanism with hours-scale persistence after stimulation stops.**

- **Garcia-Larrea L, Peyron R. "Motor cortex stimulation for neuropathic pain: From phenomenology to mechanisms." *Neuroimage*. 2007;37(Suppl 1):S71-9. PMID: 17644413. DOI: 10.1016/j.neuroimage.2007.05.062.**
  Review combining electrophysiology and functional imaging across multiple MCS pain studies. MCS triggers fast, phasic thalamic activation, followed by a **slower cascade** in medial thalamus, anterior cingulate/orbitofrontal cortex, and PAG that is **delayed relative to the cortical stimulation itself and becomes maximal in the hours following stimulation cessation** — i.e., pain relief can outlast the stimulation, and by extension plausibly also takes time to build in. **Why it matters for RCS08 specifically:** ACC and OFC — the exact regions this cohort's own sensing/decoding work (Shirvalkar et al. 2023, below) targets — are named here as the *downstream, delayed* structures in an established pain-modulation cascade, which is a mechanistic reason to expect ACC/OFC signals to lag any stimulation change by more than seconds. Limitation: MCS is a different modality (cortical surface, not deep structure) and mostly a review synthesis, not a new controlled time-course measurement.

**Spinal cord stimulation (SCS) — the only neuromodulation literature with a direct, quantified measurement of the analogous "carryover" (wash-out) phenomenon.**

- **Meier K, de Vos CC, Bordeleau M, et al. "Examining the Duration of Carryover Effect in Patients With Chronic Pain Treated With Spinal Cord Stimulation (EChO Study)." *Neuromodulation*. 2024;27(5):887-98. PMID: 38456888. DOI: 10.1016/j.neurom.2024.01.002.**
  Prospective multicenter study: patients with effective SCS turned their device off at home and reported when pain returned. **Median carryover time: 5 hours**, with wide individual variation; back pain (vs. limb), high-frequency stimulation, and higher baseline pain scores were associated with longer carryover. **Why it matters:** this is the single best-quantified number in this entire literature search for "how long does relief persist after stimulation stops" in any spinal/brain neuromodulation context for pain — hours, not minutes, and not weeks — and it comes with a formal, prospective, quantitative design rather than anecdote. If DBS analgesia shares any mechanism with SCS analgesia (both are proposed to engage descending pain-modulation systems), a multi-hour time constant is a reasonable first guess for a leaky-integrator model (Section C) to test, though it is not itself a DBS measurement. Limitation: SCS (dorsal column), not DBS; carryover (washout) measured, not wash-in (build-up) — the two need not be symmetric.

**A structurally-different-modality analogue: cumulative/spaced-dose effects in transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS).**

- **Monte-Silva K, Kuo MF, Liebetanz D, Paulus W, Nitsche MA. "Shaping the optimal repetition interval for cathodal transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS)." *J Neurophysiol*. 2010;103(4):1735-40. PMID: 20107115. DOI: 10.1152/jn.00924.2009.**
  12 healthy subjects, repeated cathodal tDCS with gaps of 0, 3, 20 minutes, 3, or 24 hours between two sessions. A single session's excitability effect lasted about 60 minutes; a second session given *during* the first session's after-effects extended benefit to ~120 minutes; sessions separated by 3-24 hours briefly reduced/reset the effect before it re-established. **Why it's included, with a caveat:** this is not DBS and not pain, but it is the cleanest controlled demonstration anywhere in neuromodulation that **whether a repeated exposure helps, does nothing, or is briefly counterproductive depends on the exact gap since the last exposure** — precisely the "dose history matters, and not always monotonically" structure this issue is asking about. Treat as a cross-modality analogy only, not as evidence about DBS or about RCS08.

### B. Does the brain's own signal show wash-in?

**Fast (seconds to about a minute) — evoked and oscillatory signals track stimulation almost immediately.**

- **Wiest C, He S, Duchet B, et al. "Evoked resonant neural activity in subthalamic local field potentials reflects basal ganglia network dynamics." *Neurobiol Dis*. 2023;178:106019. PMID: 36706929. DOI: 10.1016/j.nbd.2023.106019.**
  27 PD patients, externalized STN leads. Evoked resonant neural activity (ERNA — a brief, ringing electrical response the brain produces a few milliseconds after each stimulation pulse) has both fast and slow components **during ongoing stimulation**, consistent with short-term synaptic depression (a mechanism where repeated firing temporarily depletes the supply of neurotransmitter). Other work in this same literature (search-identified, not independently re-verified as a peer-reviewed number here) describes ERNA amplitude/frequency settling to a new steady state roughly 70 seconds into a stimulation train. **Why it matters:** this is a genuine within-minute "wash-in" of a specific electrical signature — but on a timescale of *seconds to about a minute*, three to four orders of magnitude faster than the weeks-scale clinical wash-in described in dystonia/depression/OCD (Section A). It shows the brain's electrical response to stimulation is not instantaneous even at its fastest, but gives no support for an hours-to-days component. Limitation: PD/STN, not pain; externalized-lead (short-term, in-clinic) recording, not RCS08's ambulatory Percept setting.

- **Cagle JN, Wong JK, Johnson KA, Foote KD, Okun MS, de Hemptinne C. "Suppression and Rebound of Pallidal Beta Power: Observation Using a Chronic Sensing DBS Device." *Front Hum Neurosci*. 2021;15:749567. PMID: 34566612. DOI: 10.3389/fnhum.2021.749567.**
  Single PD patient, **Medtronic Percept** system (the same device family as RCS08's), pallidal target. Current was stepped in 0.5 mA increments **every 20 seconds**, with beta-band power (13-30 Hz) computed at each step. Beta power tracked amplitude changes and correlated with bradykinesia severity within this short-step protocol; excessive stimulation caused a beta rebound. **Why it matters for RCS08 directly:** this is a titration-ladder protocol on the exact device platform BRAVO uses, and it found the LFP tracking the current change within the ladder's own short (20 s) step time — i.e., in this one case, on this measure, there was no evidence *needed* for a slow wash-in to explain the pattern seen during a clinic-speed ladder. This is a useful caution: a ladder run at clinic speed is well matched to detect a *fast* relationship and is not designed to reveal, or rule out, a slower one. Limitation: n=1, PD/GPi, motor sign (bradykinesia) not pain; does not report data at longer timescales.

**Slow (months) — the one long-timescale LFP finding in the literature, but confounded with disease trajectory and the post-surgical "stun effect."**

- **Trager MH, Koop MM, Velisar A, et al. "Subthalamic beta oscillations are attenuated after withdrawal of chronic high frequency neurostimulation in Parkinson's disease." *Neurobiol Dis*. 2016;96:22-30. PMID: 27553876. DOI: 10.1016/j.nbd.2016.08.003.**
  Using an investigational chronic-sensing implant (Activa PC+S, Percept's predecessor), STN beta power and motor scores were measured **after withdrawing 6 and then 12 months of continuous high-frequency DBS**. Beta power was similar within 14 seconds and 60 minutes of switching off (confirming the fast component seen elsewhere), **but** beta power was significantly *lower*, and off-stimulation motor scores significantly *better*, after 6 and 12 months of chronic DBS than they had been before DBS started — i.e., a slow, chronic-exposure effect that outlasted the immediate withdrawal and looked like a form of long-term circuit plasticity, not just an acute on/off effect. **Why it matters:** this is the clearest evidence I could verify that chronic stimulation exposure leaves a lasting mark on the brain's own electrical signal, on a timescale of months — structurally exactly the kind of "the longer it's been on, the more it helps, and that help outlasts a brief off period" phenomenon the PI is describing, though measured here as a shift in the disease's baseline trajectory rather than a moment-to-moment wash-in curve. Limitation: PD/STN motor signs; the effect is described by the authors themselves as plausibly reflecting long-term plasticity or altered disease progression, and could be confounded with simple continued post-implant recovery.

- **Steiner LA, Neumann WJ, Staub-Bartelt F, et al. "Subthalamic beta dynamics mirror Parkinsonian bradykinesia months after neurostimulator implantation." *Mov Disord*. 2017;32(8):1183-90. PMID: 28639263. DOI: 10.1002/mds.27068.**
  9 PD patients, LFP recordings **several months post-implantation** (deliberately after the acute "stun effect" — a temporary symptom improvement from the mechanical effect of electrode insertion itself, distinct from stimulation — had resolved). Beta-power suppression during repeated movement progressively diminished in tandem with worsening movement amplitude/frequency over the recording session, specifically in the beta band. **Why it matters, as a caution rather than as direct support:** it flags that any months-scale signal in RCS08's own device log could reflect the *stun effect wearing off*, or ordinary disease progression, rather than a stimulation-history effect — exactly the kind of confound the PI's own rule (drift is attributed to current, not time) is designed to prevent, and a reminder that "it changed slowly" is not by itself evidence of "it changed because of accumulated dose." Limitation: within-session dynamics in already-months-post-implant patients, not a direct before/after test of a stimulation change.

**A device-specific review, and the OCD ambulatory-sensing precedent for how much data slow neural signals require.**

- **Saengphatrachai W, Jimenez-Shahed J. "Current and future applications of local field potential-guided programming for Parkinson's disease with the Percept™ rechargeable neurostimulator." *Neurodegener Dis Manag*. 2024;14(5):131-47. PMID: 39344591. DOI: 10.1080/17582024.2024.2404386.**
  Review of Percept's BrainSense sensing capabilities for PD programming, including chronic (weeks-scale) recording of LFP power alongside device-logged amplitude changes, and the prospect of closed-loop stimulation on this platform. **Why it matters:** confirms, from the device-maker's own clinical literature (independent of this project's internal device documentation), that Percept's chronic timeline is explicitly built to let a clinician see how LFP power moves around a programming change over days-to-weeks — i.e., the right raw material for a wash-in search already exists in the device, even though this review does not itself report a wash-in measurement. Limitation: PD-focused review, not a primary pain or wash-in study.

- **Provenza NR, Sheth SA, Dastin-van Rijn EM, et al. "Long-term ecological assessment of intracranial electrophysiology synchronized to behavioral markers in obsessive-compulsive disorder." *Nat Med*. 2021;27(12):2154-64. PMID: 34887577. DOI: 10.1038/s41591-021-01550-z.**
  OCD patients with recording-capable DBS devices; **over 1,000 hours** of ambulatory ventral-striatum LFP were captured at home and in clinic, synchronized to disease-specific behavior, to identify candidate biomarkers of OCD symptom intensity across its naturally slow fluctuations. **Why it matters for RCS08's own data-collection plan:** this is direct precedent, in a psychiatric DBS population, that finding a neural correlate of a *slowly* fluctuating symptom required an unusually large ambulatory dataset — far more than a handful of clinic visits. If pain's relationship to accumulated stimulation dose is similarly slow, RCS08's existing REDCap stream (already many-times-daily for years) is closer in spirit to what this kind of question needs than any single titration day could be, but a purpose-built hold-and-return protocol (Section D) would still likely out-perform simply waiting for more of the same kind of data. Limitation: OCD, ventral striatum, not pain; describes a behavioral biomarker search, not a stimulation-dose model specifically.

- **Shirvalkar P, Prosky J, Chin G, et al. "First-in-human prediction of chronic pain state using intracranial neural biomarkers." *Nat Neurosci*. 2023;26(6):1090-99. PMID: 37217725. DOI: 10.1038/s41593-023-01338-z.**
  4 patients (this cohort's own prior study, using cortical electrodes in ACC and OFC, not the Percept DBS leads), ambulatory recordings over months. Chronic pain state was predicted from **sustained power changes** in OFC, which differed from the transient patterns seen during acute evoked pain. **Why it matters:** this is the most directly relevant "does the brain distinguish a slow, ongoing pain state from a fast, momentary one" finding in this cohort's own published literature, and it says yes — the signal that tracks chronic pain is a *sustained* one, not a moment-to-moment one, which is at least consistent with (though not proof of) a pain-relevant brain state that would plausibly also change slowly in response to a change in ongoing stimulation. Limitation: cortical (ACC/OFC) electrodes for sensing, not the Percept DBS leads whose current is the input variable in this issue; predicts pain state from brain activity, does not test a stimulation-dose relationship.

### C. How to model cumulative dose without modelling calendar time

**The core idea: replace "current in force right now" with a weighted history of recent current.**

A **leaky integrator** (also called an exponentially weighted moving average, EWMA) turns the raw current time series into a "dose" time series:

```
dose(t) = ∫ current(s) · exp(−(t − s)/τ) ds        [continuous form]
dose(t) = (1 − α)·dose(t−1) + α·current(t)          [discrete, one-step-lookback form, α = 1 − exp(−Δt/τ)]
```

τ (tau) is a single free parameter, the **time constant**: how long a burst of current at a given level keeps influencing the model after the current itself has moved on. τ → 0 makes `dose(t)` collapse to exactly today's `current(t)` — i.e., **the model currently in use is the special case of this model where the time constant is zero.** This nesting matters enormously for the PI's own methodological rule: fitting τ is not adding a time-varying term outside the current, it is asking "how much memory does the current effect have," which is still entirely an effect *of the current*, never of the calendar.

A **discrete-time state-space (Kalman-filter-style) formulation** is the natural generalization: treat an unobserved "accumulated stimulation state" z(t) as a hidden variable with

```
z(t+1) = a · z(t) + b · current(t) + noise      (a = exp(−Δt/τ) plays the same role as before)
pain(t) = f(z(t)) + noise
```

This is mathematically the same leaky integrator with an estimation procedure (Kalman filter / EM) that naturally produces a confidence interval on τ and can be extended to let a and b vary (e.g., different τ for onset vs. offset — many biological systems wash in and wash out at different rates, as Temperli et al. above literally showed for washout alone across four different signs).

A richer, non-parametric alternative is a **distributed-lag model**: instead of one exponential decay, fit a separate coefficient for several lag windows (e.g., 0-1h, 1-6h, 6-24h, 1-7d) and let the data determine the shape of the memory, rather than assuming a single exponential.

- **Gasparrini A, Armstrong B, Kenward MG. "Distributed lag non-linear models." *Stat Med*. 2010;29(21):2224-34. PMID: 20812303. DOI: 10.1002/sim.3940.**
  Introduces the general statistical machinery (the "cross-basis") for modeling an outcome that depends on both the *level* of a time-varying exposure and *how long ago* each part of that exposure occurred, allowing the exposure-response relationship itself to be non-linear at every lag. Built and validated on environmental-epidemiology data (temperature and mortality), not neuroscience. **Why it matters:** this is the general-purpose statistical toolkit for exactly the modeling problem in this issue — "an outcome (pain) responds to a history of an exposure (current), not just its instantaneous value, and we don't want to assume the shape of either the exposure-response curve or the lag structure in advance." A single-τ leaky integrator is the cheapest, most data-efficient special case of this general framework; a full distributed-lag model is the richest and most data-hungry. Given RCS08 is a single participant with a limited number of true setting changes (Section D), starting with the cheap single-τ model and only moving to the richer distributed-lag model if it wins out-of-sample is the right order of operations.

**Why a dose-history term is a current effect, and how to test it against "current in force."**

Because `dose(t)` is a deterministic function of the recorded current time series with no calendar-time input, adding it does not violate decisions 193-196 (time modelled nowhere; drift is a current effect) — it is a *more patient* version of the same current effect, one that is allowed to have inertia. The clean test is a **nested model comparison** inside the purged, block-of-time cross-validation already built for this project (the project's own "decision 240" framework): fit `pain ~ shape(current_in_force)` (Model A) and `pain ~ shape(dose_τ(current_history))` (Model B) for a grid of τ values including τ = 0, using the same held-out time blocks for both, and compare out-of-sample fit. If the best-scoring τ is 0 (or indistinguishable from 0 within a bootstrap interval), there is no support for wash-in beyond what the current model already captures; if a clearly non-zero τ scores better out of sample than τ = 0, that is direct, falsifiable, quantified support for a wash-in/wash-out effect, with its own estimated size.

**Identifiability traps — why this is harder than it sounds with one patient's data.**

1. **Collinearity with calendar time.** If current has only ever trended in one direction, or changed rarely, then `dose(t)` and calendar time `t` are nearly the same variable, and no amount of statistical cleverness can tell a slow current effect apart from a slow time-only drift (the exact confound the PI's rule is designed to guard against). Breaking this requires periods where current **goes up and later comes back down** to a previously visited value — exactly what a titration ladder's down-leg rows provide (see Section D, test 3) and what a single "hold this setting for months" period cannot provide on its own.

2. **Too few setting changes.** A time-constant τ can only be estimated from transitions — moments where current changed and pain was observed to respond with some delay. The project's own record has very few clean transitions to work with (per this project's chronic-log analysis, only 4 of 21 candidate left-side changes were even readable at all because of gaps in the device's chronic log and confounds from simultaneous band or contact changes). With that few transitions, τ will be estimated with a very wide, possibly unbounded, confidence interval — a result of "we can't tell" should be reported honestly as underpowered, not as "τ = 0."

3. **Short clinic steps cannot see slow effects.** A titration ladder step lasting a few minutes is, by construction, only sensitive to a *fast* component of the dose-response (seconds to low tens of minutes) — the Cagle et al. (2021) Percept case above shows LFP tracking current within a 20-second step, and Temperli et al. (2003) shows even the *slowest* PD motor sign resolves within hours, so a multi-minute clinic step genuinely can characterize the fast end of plausible time constants. It cannot see, and its silence cannot rule out, an hours-to-days component; mixing minutes-apart clinic-ladder rows with days-apart ambulatory REDCap rows into one lag-fitting exercise risks the abundant fast (clinic) data statistically overwhelming the sparse slow (ambulatory) data unless the model is deliberately built (or the streams deliberately kept separate, as this project's own two-stage clinic/REDCap architecture already does) to represent both timescales.

4. **Reverse causation from the titration process itself.** In any real titration, current is often changed *in response to* the pain or side-effect level just observed (raised when pain is high, lowered on side effects) — this is precisely the "treatment-confounder feedback" structure that motivated the development of marginal structural models in epidemiology: a variable (recent pain) that is simultaneously a *confounder* of the next treatment decision (current) and *on the causal pathway* from earlier treatment to the later outcome.
   - **Robins JM, Hernán MA, Brumback B. "Marginal structural models and causal inference in epidemiology." *Epidemiology*. 2000;11(5):550-60. PMID: 10955408. DOI: 10.1097/00001648-200009000-00011.**
   Formalizes why standard regression adjustment is *biased* when a time-varying covariate is both affected by past treatment and predictive of future treatment — exactly the loop a clinician titrating current against reported pain creates. **Why it matters:** a naive lagged regression of pain on past current, fit on titration-session data where the clinician was actively responding to pain, can be biased in either direction by this feedback loop. The safest data for estimating a dose-history effect are periods where current was held or changed for reasons **unrelated to that visit's own emerging pain trajectory** — e.g., a fixed-length research ladder step, or a safety-ceiling-driven change — rather than free titration.

### D. Testable predictions in RCS08's data — concrete analyses

Each of the following can, in principle, be run on data this project already has (device-derived `therapy_settings`, REDCap NRS/VAS, the chronic band-power log, and the existing purged time-blocked cross-validation and `CovariateShape` machinery), listed roughly cheapest-first.

1. **Nested τ comparison on the ambulatory (REDCap) stream.** Data needed: current-in-force per pain report (already assembled per the project's own stimulation-current reader) and the pain score itself. Analysis: grid τ over {0, 10 min, 1 h, 6 h, 1 day, 3 days, 7 days}; for each τ, build `dose_τ(t)` and fit `pain ~ shape(dose_τ)` inside the existing purged, block-of-time cross-validation; report out-of-sample R²/log-likelihood per τ with a bootstrap interval. **Supports wash-in:** the best τ is clearly greater than 0 and out-performs τ = 0 by more than a shuffled-current null. **Refutes it:** the cross-validation curve is flat or peaks at τ = 0.

2. **Check the specific three-block finding for a mundane alternative explanation first.** Before treating the 1.51 → 0.53 → −0.06 finding at the unchanged L1.6/R1.2 mA setting as evidence of wash-in, check whether current was still *rising toward* 1.6/1.2 mA in the period immediately before that "unchanged" window began. If so, the observed fall could simply be the tail end of the leaky integrator's dose still climbing to its ceiling under the *old* model of instantaneous current — a cheap, high-value check using data the project already has, and the most parsimonious first thing to rule out.

3. **Up-leg versus down-leg hysteresis in the ladder.** Data needed: the ladder's already-recorded rising and falling steps at matched currents. Analysis: at each current value visited on both the way up and the way down within a session, compare the pain/objective reading. **Supports wash-in with a meaningful τ relative to ladder-step length:** readings at a given current are systematically *better* on the down-leg than the up-leg (residual benefit from the recently higher current has not yet washed out) — a direct, within-session hysteresis signature that does not require waiting for ambulatory data at all. **Refutes it:** no systematic up/down difference at matched currents.

4. **Apply the same τ grid to the chronic device band-power log.** Data needed: the 10-minute-epoch chronic LFP log around each of the (currently 4) readable left-side setting changes. Analysis: compare a step-function model of band power against the leaky-integrator model, per τ, for goodness of fit. **Caveat to state up front:** with only 4 usable transitions this is likely underpowered to pin down τ precisely; treat any result as hypothesis-generating, not confirmatory, and say so plainly if reported.

5. **Re-run the surrogate/calibration diagnosis with dose in place of instantaneous current.** The 55 Hz/60-160 µs pain-objective surface has already been flagged (in this project's own record) as "moves between blocks of time," with 62% of its held-out miss shared by whole time blocks. Refit that same stratum using `dose_τ` instead of raw current-in-force as the input. **Supports wash-in as the explanation for that movement:** the between-block-shared fraction of the miss drops substantially. **Argues for something else (a real unmodelled time effect, or a different confound such as the already-documented weekend effect):** it does not drop.

6. **Design the next titration/exploratory ladder with deliberate hold-and-return steps.** Rather than only monotonic up/down legs, include steps that raise current, hold for multiple days at home, then deliberately return to a previously visited lower current and hold again — this is the data structure that best identifies τ (breaks the current/time collinearity, per identifiability trap 1) and should be the single highest-value change to future data collection if this question is a priority.

### E. Implications for titration sessions and for closed-loop stimulation

**Clinic titration (minutes per step).** By the evidence above, a multi-minute ladder step is well matched to the *fast* end of any plausible dose-response (seconds to perhaps a few tens of minutes — Temperli et al. 2003's fastest PD signs, Cagle et al. 2021's 20-second Percept steps) and is not capable of detecting, or ruling out, a slower component. **A titration finding of "no relationship" or "flat" at a given setting therefore only means the fast component is flat — it says nothing about a possible slow one.** The existing two-part protocol (a fast up/down ladder, plus short multi-minute holds) captures the fast component well; if a genuinely slow wash-in exists on the scale suggested by the dystonia/depression/OCD literature (weeks) or even the levodopa long-duration-response analogy (days), **no clinic-day protocol, however cleverly sequenced, can measure it** — only the ambulatory stream, ideally with deliberate hold-and-return steps (Section D, item 6), can.

**How long to hold a new home setting before judging it.** No pain-DBS study has established a specific number for this, so any recommendation here is an extrapolation, not a citation-backed fact. By analogy to the dystonia and depression literature (benefit still accruing, and confirmed by blinded assessment, at 3 months, with further change out to 6 months — Kupsch et al. 2006; Ruge et al. 2011) and to the levodopa long-duration response (a benefit whose own half-life is on the order of days — Nutt & Holford 1996), a reasonable **hedged** starting point is: do not draw a firm conclusion from less than 1-2 weeks at a genuinely new setting, and continue to reassess for up to about 3 months before calling a setting's ceiling effect "reached." This is an analogy-based estimate, not a pain-DBS-specific measurement, and RCS08's own data (Section D, item 1) can and should be used to replace it with a fitted number.

**Closed-loop (adaptive) stimulation.** This is the sharpest practical stake in the PI's question. Adaptive DBS systems built to date (e.g., Little et al. 2013's beta-triggered controller, below) turn stimulation on and off on a scale of seconds to minutes, tuned to track a *fast* biomarker:

- **Little S, Pogosyan A, Neal S, et al. "Adaptive deep brain stimulation in advanced Parkinson disease." *Ann Neurol*. 2013;74(3):449-57. PMID: 23852650. DOI: 10.1002/ana.23951.**
  8 PD patients; a beta-power-triggered controller turned STN stimulation on and off in real time. Motor scores improved 50% (blinded) versus continuous stimulation, using 56% less stimulation time. **Why it matters as a contrast case:** this is the strongest evidence that a fast controller *can* work — but critically, it works for a sign (bradykinesia/rigidity) whose own washout, per Temperli et al. 2003, resolves within tens of minutes, i.e., the controller's timescale and the symptom's own physiological timescale are matched. If RCS08's pain relief instead needs hours-to-days to build in, a controller cycling on that PD-motor timescale would, by construction, almost never hold a setting long enough to reach anywhere near the benefit a sustained setting could produce, and would be likely to (wrongly) read "no effect" for a mechanism that does work under sustained exposure. This is not a criticism of the adaptive-DBS approach in general — it is a mismatch-of-timescales risk specific to applying a fast controller to a possibly slow symptom.

The OCD ambulatory-sensing work (Provenza et al. 2021, above) is the field's clearest acknowledgment of this same tension in a psychiatric, plausibly-slow-symptom context: finding any biomarker of OCD's own slow symptom fluctuations required **over 1,000 hours** of continuous sensing, and current adaptive-DBS thinking for slow psychiatric symptoms has moved toward a two-timescale architecture — a slow outer loop that adjusts a target dose over hours-to-days, wrapped around a fast inner loop that only manages moment-to-moment safety. **For RCS08, this suggests that a fast-biomarker search that has so far failed to beat its own out-of-sample null (as this project's own analysis has found, testing 1-60 seconds of signal against instantaneous current) may be asking an electrophysiologically fast signal to explain a behaviorally slow effect** — the same bands might do better against a leaky-integrated (dosed) version of both the neural signal and the current, rather than against either one's instantaneous value, and that is a natural next analysis (Section D, item 1, applied to the neural signal as well as to pain) rather than a reason to conclude the bands carry no pain-relevant information at all.
