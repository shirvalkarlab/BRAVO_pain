## The questions

Two separate leads sit in this patient's own data, and one visit design can address both.

**(a) The zero-current lead.** In the five weeks after the implant (2025-07-16 to 08-22), both sides were programmed at 0 mA. During that window, 96 pain reports were filed; 75 of them could be matched to a recording taken sometime the same day, on 25 separate days. Within that group, on the left sensing pair (contacts 1 and 3, written L 1-3+), band power at 21.5 and 22.5 Hz rose with the 0-100 pain scale (VAS): a rank correlation of +0.38 at 21.5 Hz and +0.35 at 22.5 Hz, both surviving the correction this project applies for testing 22 bands at once (q = 0.044). A neighbouring left pair (L 0-2+) showed the same pattern at 21.5 Hz (+0.36) and 22.5 Hz (+0.40, q = 0.022). This is the one current-free window with real data behind it; two later current-free windows (April and May 2026) had only device-triggered snapshots on the right side, no streamed recordings on the left, and did not repeat the finding. **The catch:** of the 75 matched pairs, only 13-17 had a recording within 60 minutes of the report; the typical gap was 5.1 hours. A pain report and a recording five hours apart can drift independently for reasons that have nothing to do with each other, so this correlation is a lead worth confirming with tightly-timed pairs, not yet a settled finding.

**(b) The harmonic question.** The left lead is currently stimulated at 55 pulses per second (55 Hz). Two independent physical processes can put a false signal at 27.5 Hz (half of 55) that looks exactly like a real 27.5 Hz brain rhythm: (1) simple electrical aliasing of the stimulation pulse train, which this project's own device notes already compute lands at 25 and 30 Hz for a 55 Hz rate; and (2) a documented biological phenomenon in which the brain's own gamma-range activity locks onto exactly half the stimulation rate ("1:2 entrainment"), described in Parkinson's disease by several groups. Both processes place a false signal inside the same 21.5-27.5 Hz family that (a) found rising with pain. If either explanation is right, changing the pulse rate — while holding the current and the pulse width fixed — should move or collapse the elevated power near 24.5-27.5 Hz, because the false signal always follows the stimulator's rate, not the brain's own.

**(c) Identifying whether stimulation "history" matters** — whether the pain relief from a given current setting builds in in over hours to days, or lingers after the setting is changed, in a way this project's current model (which only looks at whatever current is programmed right now) is missing. This project already tested memory of past current at seven different lengths (0 hours up to 14 days) against 777 numeric pain ratings and 612 Left Leg VAS ratings: memory never predicted pain better than the current programmed right now, and up to about 3 days of memory was no worse (within the uncertainty of the test); memory beyond about 7 days was clearly worse. That result rules out long memory but cannot rule out a short one (hours to a few days), because it was built from currents that were rarely held and then deliberately returned to a prior level — exactly the kind of design that would let a short memory effect be seen.

## What the record already says

**Settings currently in force (all sourced from this project's own decision log; every number below is quoted, not estimated).** The left lead stimulates through a single contact, contact 2 (written "L C+2-" in the clinic-sheet notation); the device's own rule for which two contacts may sense (they must be the two contacts flanking the stimulating one) makes L 1-3+ the only sensing pair the left lead currently allows. The right lead stimulates through two contacts, 1 and 2 together ("R C+1-2-"), which makes R 0-3+ its only allowed sensing pair. Both facts, and the rule itself, are recorded in decision 217 and confirmed again in decision 247. The stimulation rate in force on both sides is 55 Hz (decisions 233, 236). The pulse-width pairing in force is 100 microseconds on the left and 150 on the right (decision 236, confirmed again in decision 255, which shows this pairing still active as of 2026-09-24). The PI-set safety limit is 4.5 mA on each side (decisions 145, 160); nothing in this design goes near it.

**Why the harmonic worry is not hypothetical.** This project's own device reference document computes, from first principles, which multiples of the stimulation rate fold back into the usable frequency range at the device's 250-samples-per-second recording rate. For 55 Hz, the folded values land at 5, 25 and 30 Hz, which contaminates eight of the 22 usable bands, including all of 22.5 through 29.5 Hz — precisely the family that (a) found rising with pain (`DEVICE_percept_rc.md` §9). Separately, decision 236 computes where exactly-half the stimulation rate falls: 27.5 Hz, which sits inside the 5 Hz width of the 25.5, 26.5 and 27.5 Hz bands, leaving only 24.5 Hz clear of that particular contamination. The project's own document records a concrete example of how large this can be: on one visit, the two bands that contain 55 Hz itself reached 7,544 and 23,121 device power units, and before that was caught, the single largest apparent effect anywhere in this patient's data (45,506 units, an 18-fold rise) turned out to be exactly this — the stimulator, not the brain. On today's stored grid, every band that currently "qualifies" as tracking pain sits on one of these stimulator-related frequencies (decision 220), and neither L 0-2+ nor L 1-3+ currently has a band that clears the stricter bar of also falling with current (the one-band rule, decision 199).

**What this project's own confound checks already found.** A held-out-in-time test that removes the current's own contribution before asking whether a band still predicts pain (decisions 240, 241, corrected in decision 262c because the first version of this test used a 60-second matching window rather than the platform's normal 60-minute one) finds: at 60 minutes of matching, on L 1-3+, the current alone predicts pain fairly well (a discrimination score of 0.708 with 60 seconds of signal), all bands together do a little better (0.743), and with the current statistically taken out, the bands still carry something beyond the current at 60 seconds of signal (0.683, outside the range expected by chance, p = 0.04, not corrected for the several signal lengths tried) but not at 30 seconds. The right side (R 0-3+) shows nothing beyond chance at either length. This is a second, independent reason (alongside (a)) to look specifically at the left side, and it is exactly the kind of result that a genuinely current-free test — no current running at all — would settle more cleanly than a statistical removal can.

**What a fresh current-free window would gain that the original one could not.** The original 0 mA window sat in the first 12 weeks after surgery. A large literature (below) shows that the tissue around a new DBS lead is still settling for weeks to months regardless of whether current is delivered — meaning that window carried a second confound on top of the current one. A new current-free window planned for more than a year after implant would not carry that confound, and is in that specific sense a *cleaner* test than the one already in the data, not merely a repeat of it.

## Evidence and literature

Everything below was checked against PubMed, the publisher, or (for the AHRQ guide) the agency's own site; anything not independently confirmed is listed under "Unverified" at the end rather than stated as fact.

### On carry-over and wash-out (how long a stimulation effect lingers, or takes to appear)

Spinal cord stimulation gives the best-measured number for how long relief lingers after stimulation is switched off, and it is the closest published analogue to what this design needs. **Meier et al. (2024, *Neuromodulation*, PMID 38456888)** had patients whose spinal cord stimulators were already working turn the devices off at home and report when their pain returned: the median time before pain came back was **5 hours**, with wide differences between people, and back pain, high-frequency stimulation and higher starting pain were all linked to a longer lingering effect. This is spinal, not brain, stimulation, but it is the single best-quantified number available for "how long should we wait after turning stimulation off before we trust that we are measuring the off state."

For Parkinson's DBS specifically, **Temperli et al. (2003, *Neurology*, PMID 12525722)** switched stimulation off in 35 patients and timed how fast different signs came back: tremor worsened within minutes, slower movement and muscle stiffness worsened over roughly half an hour to an hour, and posture/gait signs worsened slowest, over three to four hours, with 90% of the total worsening reached only after two hours. Turning stimulation back on reversed this faster than the switch-off had. The lesson that matters here is not the specific numbers (this is a different disease and a different symptom) but the finding that **one device, one lead, four different signs, four different speeds** — a caution against assuming this patient's pain, or any one band of brain activity, will settle on the same clock as any of these.

This project's own analysis (decision 262b) already tested memory lengths of 0 hours to 14 days against pain and found no support for memory beyond what today's current already explains, with the clearest break between 3 days (no detectably worse) and 7 days (clearly worse). Combined with Meier's 5-hour median and Temperli's up-to-4-hour range, the honest read is: **a fast (minutes-to-hours) lingering effect cannot be ruled out by anything this project has measured so far, and the literature says such an effect, if present, most likely resolves somewhere between about an hour and about half a day.** That is the number this design should be built to catch, not a number that has already been shown to exist.

### On whether the stimulator itself can manufacture the signal being watched

**Sermon, Starr, Denison & Duchet (2024, *Brain Stimulation*, DOI 10.1016/j.brs.2024.02.017)** built a computer model of subthalamic brain activity under Parkinson's DBS and found that a stimulator can lock a brain rhythm to exactly half its own pulse rate ("1:2 entrainment"), but only when a real, pre-existing rhythm is already there for the pulse train to grab onto; without a pre-existing rhythm, stimulation can still produce extra activity in that frequency range, but it will not sit at exactly half the rate and will not track a change in the rate. This is a modelling paper, not a recording from a person, but it makes a specific, testable prediction: **if this patient's 24.5-27.5 Hz activity is being manufactured by the stimulator, changing the rate should move it; if it is a real, independent rhythm, changing the rate should leave it where it is.**

**Mathiopoulou et al. (2025, *Nature Communications*, DOI 10.1038/s41467-025-58132-7)** recorded 19 people with Parkinson's disease and found this same half-rate locking in 15 of them during high-frequency subthalamic stimulation, and — importantly — the locked signal's strength tracked how well the patient was moving, meaning a stimulator-locked signal is not automatically meaningless; it can still carry real clinical information, but only once it has been checked against an independent measure of the state it is supposed to track. This project has not yet run that check for any of this patient's candidate bands.

**Swann et al. (2016, *Journal of Neuroscience*, PMID 27307233)** recorded two people with Parkinson's disease and drug-induced involuntary movements using a fully implanted, chronic-sensing device (the direct predecessor of the platform this patient's own Percept RC extends) and found a narrow-band gamma rhythm, around 60-90 Hz in the movement-control part of the brain and a weaker, matching one in the deep brain-stimulation target itself, that appeared specifically during the movement disorder and was strongly linked in timing between the two sites. This paper does not itself test a rate swap, but it is the earliest chronic-recording demonstration that a narrow, stimulation-adjacent gamma rhythm can appear and disappear with a clinical state in exactly this kind of implant, which is why later work (Sermon, Mathiopoulou) went looking for, and found, its relationship to the stimulator's own rate.

**Hammer, Kochanski, Starr & Little (2022, *Stereotactic and Functional Neurosurgery*, DOI 10.1159/000521431)** characterised the specific recording glitches this device family produces: heartbeat noise in some contact configurations, and — the one that matters directly for a rate-swap test — a **brief distortion of the low-frequency content every time a stimulation setting is changed.** Their own practical advice is to record a short stimulation-off baseline whenever a clean read is needed, and, by direct extension, to expect and discard a short window right after any parameter change rather than trust it.

This project's own literature review already assembled the wider Parkinson's evidence that a single band, single switching-value controller (Little et al. 2013; Velisar et al. 2019; the large ADAPT-PD trial, Stanslaski et al. 2024 and Bronte-Stewart et al. 2025) works reasonably well for a rhythm that (i) genuinely falls when current rises, (ii) changes on a timescale the device's fastest reaction (a quarter of a second) or slowest built-in ramp (five minutes) can actually track, and (iii) has been checked for exactly the kind of stimulator-locked contamination this section describes. None of this patient's candidate bands has cleared all three bars yet (`artifacts/research_2026-09-24_3_tonic_current_and_single_threshold.md`, already in this project's files).

### On N-of-1 (single-patient, repeated-crossover) trial design

**Kravitz RL, Duan N, eds., DEcIDE Methods Center N-of-1 Guidance Panel. "Design and Implementation of N-of-1 Trials: A User's Guide." AHRQ Publication No. 13(14)-EHC122-EF. Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality, January 2014.** This is the standard methods reference for testing a treatment in one person, repeatedly, rather than across many people once. Its core design elements, all directly applicable here: repeat the comparison in **multiple treatment periods** (commonly at least three), not just once, because a single comparison cannot rule out a one-off coincidence; **randomise which condition comes first** within each pair of periods, so a steady drift in the underlying condition cannot be mistaken for a real difference; use a **washout** between periods when the treatment's effect is fast and reversible (this is the part of the design that does not transfer cleanly here — see below); and analyse the whole series together rather than any one comparison alone.

**Kravitz RL, Schmid CH, Marois M, et al. "Effect of Mobile Device-Supported Single-Patient Multi-crossover Trials on Treatment of Chronic Musculoskeletal Pain: A Randomized Clinical Trial." *JAMA Intern Med.* 2018;178(10):1368-1377. PMID 30193253.** A randomised trial of this exact design style, applied to chronic pain patients directly (albeit comparing oral medications, not stimulation settings): repeated, randomly-ordered treatment pairs, tracked on a phone app, showed the single-patient design was usable and informative in a real chronic-pain population, and is the closest published precedent for running this kind of design on an ordinary pain patient rather than in a research lab.

**Mendonça M, Cotovio G, Barbosa R, Grunho M, Oliveira-Maia AJ. "An Argument in Favor of Deep Brain Stimulation for Uncommon Movement Disorders: The Case for N-of-1 Trials in Holmes Tremor." *Front Hum Neurosci.* 2022;16:921523. PMID 35782038.** This paper argues, for a different movement disorder, that DBS programming is naturally suited to the N-of-1 design because the "treatment" (a stimulation setting) can be turned on, off, and switched back with the same device, in the same patient, without waiting for a new prescription — exactly the situation an implanted stimulator like this patient's own creates, and exactly the situation this design exploits.

**What does not transfer cleanly.** The classic N-of-1 washout assumes a fast, fully reversible effect (like a pill wearing off). This project's own data (decision 262b, above) show current's effect on pain is *not* obviously fast and fully reversible: a short memory (hours to a few days) cannot be ruled out. So a rigid, short washout between conditions would risk exactly the confound the guide is meant to prevent — measuring a lingering effect from the previous condition and mistaking it for the new one. The rate-swap test (part (b) below) sidesteps this because the current never changes, only the pulse rate, and the physical mechanisms in question (electrical aliasing, half-rate locking) are documented to settle in seconds, not hours (Hammer 2022's parameter-change glitch; the general finding across this literature that oscillatory locking to a stimulator tracks the stimulator within one to a few pulse cycles). The current-change parts of this design (the 0 mA block and the hold-and-return sequence) are built around *measuring* the lingering effect directly, rather than assuming a washout length and hoping it is long enough.

### On why an early, current-free window is not automatically a clean test (bearing on how the new one should be read)

**Hamani et al. (2024, *Brain Communications*, DOI 10.1093/braincomms/fcae093)** reviewed the "insertional effect" — the fact that inserting a DBS lead, on its own, before any stimulation, changes both the symptom and the tissue around the electrode for weeks to months, through mechanisms (local swelling, immune response, scarring) that have nothing to do with current. In the PI's own earlier chronic-pain cohort, roughly 43% of patients had a large pain drop from the lead placement alone, before stimulation ever started (**Shirvalkar et al. 2020, *J Clin Med.*, DOI 10.3390/jcm9103155** — already cited and verified in this project's own literature files). **Peng et al. (2024, *Front Cell Dev Biol.*, DOI 10.3389/fcell.2024.1370287)** and **Tani et al. (2026, *Neurol Med Chir.*, DOI 10.2176/jns-nmc.2025-0102)** show the recorded brain signal itself, not just the symptom, keeps changing for weeks to months after implant in Parkinson's disease, for the same tissue-settling reasons. None of these three papers is about this patient's target or about pain, but together they explain exactly why decision 262(a)'s finding is called "a lead to confirm," not an established result: the first 12 weeks after implant carry a second, non-electrical confound that a brand-new current-free block, run more than a year later, would not carry.

## The protocol

All of this needs the PI's explicit sign-off before anything is programmed; nothing here writes to the device, and this document is a design for the clinician to review and, if approved, carry out by hand on the tablet.

### Visit preparation (before the visit)

1. Confirm the settings in force have not changed since decision 255 (55 Hz, 100/150 microsecond pulse widths, L C+2-, R C+1-2-, sensing on L 1-3+ and R 0-3+).
2. Decide with the clinical team whether the left lead's always-on chronic power log (a running 10-minute average that never stops, whatever the sensing centre is set to) should be re-pointed to 24.5 Hz — the one band in the 21.5-27.5 Hz family that this project's own harmonic math says is clear of the 55 Hz half-rate contamination (decision 236) — for the duration of any take-home portion of this design (part (c) below). This project's own device notes describe a sensing-only mode ("Single Threshold Inverse") that logs a band without ever changing stimulation, which is the safest way to do this if the firmware allows it; confirming that this mode does not disturb the ordinary, already-programmed continuous stimulation is a question for the clinical/device team, not something this document can answer.
3. Prepare the same clinic-sheet format already used for this patient's ladders (ramp-and-test rows, 1.0 mA steps on the way down, both-sides-together corners, a row for every rating — decisions 160, 181, 230), extended with the new rows below.
4. Agree in advance, with the clinical team, on a rescue plan: at what pain level or distress level the visit stops early and stimulation is restored, regardless of where the protocol has reached.

### Part 1 — a tightly-timed, current-free block (answers question (a))

**Purpose.** Repeat the original 0 mA correlation with recordings taken minutes, not hours, from each rating, so a positive or negative result is not explained by the two things having simply drifted apart over the gap.

| Step | Time | What happens | What is recorded |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 0:00 | Both sides ramped down to 0 mA (standard device ramp; this project's own data show a ramp typically completes in about 2 seconds and, in the most extreme case measured, in 75 seconds for a very large multi-step change, so a short pause after the ramp is enough before anything else happens) | Time stimulation reached 0 mA on each side |
| 2 | 0:00-1:30 | Waiting period. No counted measurements yet. | A rating and one recording anyway, timestamped, so this period is not simply thrown away if something useful shows up in it |
| 3 | 1:30 onward, every 20-30 minutes, for as long as the visit and the patient's comfort allow (aim for at least 3 hours of counted time) | One pain rating (the existing clinic-sheet NRS and VAS items) immediately followed, within 1-2 minutes, by one on-demand streamed recording on both L 1-3+ and R 0-3+ (this device product records the raw voltage and, unlike the chronic log, is not locked to one band) | Rating, recording start time, minutes-since-off-began |
| 4 | End of the counted period | Stimulation ramped back up to the settings in force, watching for comfort | Time stimulation resumed |

**Why the 1.5-hour wait, and why it is not treated as a real washout.** Meier et al. (2024) found a *median* of 5 hours before spinal-cord-stimulation relief wore off at home; Temperli et al. (2003) found the slowest Parkinson's DBS signs took up to about 4 hours. A 1.5-hour wait only guarantees the *fastest* part of any lingering effect has passed, not all of it. Rather than pretending a longer wait solves this, the design keeps recording pain and band power all the way through the block and stamps every point with minutes-since-off, so if there is a slow fade, it shows up as a trend inside the data rather than being hidden by an arbitrary cut point.

**Analysis, using tools this project already has.** Compute the same rank correlation between each of the 22 bands and pain that produced the original finding, but restricted to this new, tightly-matched set; use the block-bootstrap-over-days method this project already uses for its grid cells (decision 183) for the confidence interval, correct for the 22 bands with the same method already in use (decision 63), and report the pre-registered bands (L 1-3+ and L 0-2+ at 21.5 and 22.5 Hz — four bands, chosen because they are the ones already found) separately from an honest, uncorrected look at all 22 as a discovery pass. Also read the same four bands' relationship to pain during a recent, stable, current-on stretch (this needs no new data collection — it is already computed as part of the current-adjusted grid, decision 234), so the visit answers both "is this real without current" and "does it still show up with current on" side by side.

### Part 2 — the rate swap, 55 Hz against 60 Hz (answers question (b))

**Purpose.** Test whether the elevated power in the 24.5-27.5 Hz family follows the stimulator's own rate (evidence it is manufactured by the stimulator) or stays in place when the rate changes (evidence against that specific explanation — it does not, by itself, prove the signal is about pain).

**Why 60 Hz and not, say, 110 Hz.** This project's own device notes already worked this out: 110 Hz changes the rate by a factor of two, which changes a great deal about how the pulses land in tissue as well as where their harmonics fall; 60 Hz changes almost nothing except exactly where the stimulator's own frequency footprint sits (`DEVICE_percept_rc.md` §9). Sitting the comparison rate as close as possible to 55 Hz, while moving its harmonic footprint cleanly out of the family being watched, isolates the one variable this test is about.

This part does not need stimulation off — it can be done at the current already in force, or at a fixed, tolerated test current within the 4.5 mA limit, chosen with the clinician. Only the pulse rate changes; current and pulse width are held fixed throughout.

| Condition | Rate | Duration | Recordings | Rating |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A1 | 55 Hz (in force) | 10 min | 2-3 on-demand streams on L 1-3+ (and R 0-3+ for comparison) | 2-3 ratings |
| — transition — | reprogram to 60 Hz | discard first 30-60 s | (excluded, per Hammer et al. 2022's finding that a parameter change briefly distorts low-frequency content) | — |
| B1 | 60 Hz | 10 min | same as A1 | same as A1 |
| — transition — | reprogram back to 55 Hz | discard first 30-60 s | (excluded) | — |
| A2 | 55 Hz | 10 min | same as A1 | same as A1 |

If time allows, repeat this A-B-A sequence once more (a second cycle), which is worth doing for two reasons the N-of-1 literature gives directly: it protects against a one-off coincidence, and it lets the order be effectively counterbalanced (A-B-A then B-A-B) rather than always starting on the same rate. If only one cycle is completed, say so plainly in the write-up, following this project's own house rule (decision 237: "a lead to repeat, never an established result").

**What to compare.** For each condition: the power specifically at 24.5 Hz (the one band this project's own math says is clear of the 55 Hz half-rate contamination) against the power at 25.5-27.5 Hz (contaminated at 55 Hz) and against a new check near 29.5-30 Hz (where the contamination should move to under 60 Hz, since half of 60 is 30). A signal that is purely stimulator-driven should show the elevated region move from the 25.5-27.5 Hz group toward 29.5-30 Hz when the rate changes to 60 Hz, and 24.5 Hz should look about the same in both conditions either way. A signal that stays exactly where it was, in the same three bands, regardless of rate, is not explained by either of the two stimulator-driven mechanisms in the evidence section above (though it still is not, by itself, proof that the signal is about pain — that is a separate question, already being asked by the current-removal work in decisions 234, 240-241).

### Part 3 — a hold-and-return sequence, started at the visit, continued at home (answers question (c))

**Purpose.** This project's own memory-length test (decision 262b) could not rule out a short (hours-to-days) lingering effect, because the currents it tested were rarely held and then deliberately returned to. A hold-and-return design fixes exactly that gap: hold a current, move to a different one, then come back to the first one, and see whether pain (and the chronic-logged band, if re-pointed per the preparation step above) reads the same both times.

**Why 3-4 day holds.** Decision 262(b)'s own results are the reason for this choice: memory of the current up to about 3 days was no worse than using today's current alone; memory of 7 days or more was clearly worse. A 3-4 day hold sits right at the edge that existing data could not resolve either way, so it is the length most likely to be informative rather than the length most likely to look flat regardless of the true answer.

A possible schedule for the left side (the side with the pain-linked bands and the drift already flagged in decision 253), agreed with the clinician for a specific starting current X within the safe range:

| Step | Left current | Length | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | X (current in force) | 3-4 days | baseline hold |
| 2 | X + a clinically comfortable step (agreed with the clinician; well inside the 4.5 mA ceiling) | 3-4 days | "up" |
| 3 | X (return) | 3-4 days | tests whether pain/the band reads the same as step 1 |
| 4 | X - a comparable step down, if tolerated | 3-4 days | "down" |
| 5 | X (return again) | 3-4 days | second confirmation |

The patient (and any caregiver making the change) is kept blind to which numbered step is in force, following the same practice already designed for this project's other exploratory current sequences (decision 230); a coordinator or clinician, not the patient, keeps the key. Pain ratings continue on the existing clinic-sheet/REDCap schedule — no new burden there. The left chronic power log (already always running, at whatever band it is pointed to) supplies the neural side with no extra visits needed, provided the re-pointing in the preparation step is approved.

**Analysis.** At the next follow-up, compare step 1 against step 3, and step 3 against step 5, at the *same* nominal current, using the paired-comparison logic this project's literature review already laid out for its own recorded up-and-down current runs (a real difference between a first visit and a later return to the same current is the signature a lingering effect would leave); and re-run the existing memory-length grid (decision 262b's method, now on data purpose-built to answer the question) on the new, controlled sequence rather than only on the messy, clinician-driven history used before.

## Sample size and power from the record's own numbers

This project's own file (the 2026-09-24 synthesis, section 4a) already computed how large a correlation could be reliably found (80% power, a 0.05 significance level) from the original 25 independent days: about 0.53 for one band tested alone, about 0.68 once corrected for testing all 22 bands. I checked that calculation by reproducing it with the same method (the Fisher z-transform approach for a correlation's sampling spread) and it matches exactly (0.53 and 0.68, to two decimal places), so the method below uses the same approach, solved the other way around: **given the effect size already observed (about 0.38), how many independent days would be needed to find it reliably?**

| What is being tested | Independent days needed for 80% power at a 0.05 significance level |
|---|---|
| One pre-registered band, tested alone | about 52 |
| The four pre-registered bands (two centres x two sensing pairs), corrected for testing four things | about 73 |
| A fresh 22-band discovery pass, corrected for all 22 | about 98 |

These are calculations done for this report, using the identical method the project's own file already used and verified against its own printed numbers (25 days -> 0.53 and 0.68); they are not numbers pulled from a file, and they should be read as a planning estimate, not a promise.

**What this means for the visit design.** A single visit, however densely sampled within the day, still counts as **one** independent day under this project's own convention (pain reports and recordings from the same day are not independent of each other, because the same person's pain and the same slice of brain state carry over from hour to hour within a day — the same reasoning behind the project's own day-level block-bootstrap, decision 183). Reaching 52-98 independent days would need the dense, tightly-matched design in Part 1 repeated at roughly 50 to 100 separate future visits — not achievable in any realistic near-term plan. The honest framing, consistent with this project's own language for its titration findings (decision 237: "a lead to repeat, never an established result"), is:

- **One visit** gives a much better-matched (minutes, not hours) replication of the sign and rough size of the original finding, plus a within-day estimate with its own (necessarily wide) interval — useful, but still one data point at the between-day level that actually carries statistical weight.
- **Three visits** (the AHRQ guide's usual minimum number of repeated periods for a single-patient design) would let the sign be checked for consistency across three independent days and would start to narrow the interval, without coming close to formal 80%-power confirmation.
- **Reaching the 52-98 day range** is not a realistic target through clinic visits alone; if this question is judged important enough to settle definitively, it would need either many more visits over a long stretch of time, or a way to gather same-day, tightly-matched pairs during ordinary (not off-current) days as well — a design question for the PI, not something this report can decide.

For Part 2 (the rate swap), a single 10-minute block on each side of an A-B-A cycle is a small sample by any standard; treat any single-cycle comparison as exploratory, and report a point estimate and interval rather than a bare pass/fail. A second full cycle, if time allows, roughly doubles the amount of within-visit data and is worth the extra 20-30 minutes it costs.

For Part 3 (hold-and-return), the design gives, at best, two paired within-subject comparisons (step 1 vs. step 3, step 3 vs. step 5) from one pass through the schedule — informative about the *sign* of a lingering effect if one is large, but not enough on its own to pin down its size; repeating the whole schedule at a later visit, exactly as the N-of-1 literature recommends, is the way to build confidence rather than trying to make one pass do that work.

---

## Safety and burden

- **The 4.5 mA ceiling** (decisions 145, 160) is never approached by anything in this design; Part 2's rate swap and Part 3's steps are all planned well inside it.
- **The 0 mA block (Part 1)** asks this patient to go without their programmed stimulation for several hours. This project's own data show both sides were already at 0 mA for five continuous weeks after implant, with severe pain throughout (81% of ratings at 8 or 9 out of 10) and no recorded safety event tied to the current level itself — but that period was more than a year ago, and this patient's current health, function and psychological state need a fresh clinical review before repeating any part of it, however briefly. An explicit stop rule (pain level, distress level, or the patient's own request) and a plan to restore stimulation immediately if needed should be agreed before the visit starts, not improvised during it.
- **The rate swap (Part 2)** uses two ordinary, low, well-tolerated pulse rates and does not change current or pulse width; this is a low-risk programming action in itself, but this document cannot certify it is appropriate for this patient's specific investigational permission — that sign-off belongs to the PI and the clinical team, consistent with this project's own rule that plan approval is not the same as execution authority.
- **The take-home hold-and-return (Part 3)** changes this patient's programmed current for multiple days at a time, away from their usual setting, without the patient knowing which step they are on. This carries a real risk of a pain flare that outlasts the visit itself, and needs its own explicit safety and rescue plan, agreed with the clinical team, before it starts — not folded into the general visit sign-off.
- **Burden.** The full in-clinic day (Parts 1 and 2 together, roughly 4-6 hours) is long for a patient with severe chronic pain. Splitting Part 1 (the 0 mA block) and Part 2 (the rate swap) across two separate, shorter visits is a reasonable way to reduce single-day burden, at the cost of one extra visit.

## Feasibility risks

1. **On-demand streamed recordings almost certainly need the clinician's own tablet and wand**, not the patient's take-home controller, based on how this device family is generally used in the clinic (this is my own inference from ordinary Percept RC practice and the device product table in `DEVICE_percept_rc.md` §3, not something the project's files state as a limitation outright, so it needs confirming with the clinical/device team). If true, this means every densely-sampled recording-and-rating pair in Parts 1 and 2 must happen in clinic, and only the chronic, one-band, 10-minute-average log is available for the take-home portion of Part 3.
2. **The chronic log only ever carries one band per side at a time** (a device fact, `DEVICE_percept_rc.md` §3). Re-pointing the left chronic log to 24.5 Hz for Part 3 means giving up whatever band it is currently logging; confirm this trade-off is acceptable before doing it.
3. **A pain flare that outlasts the visit** is a real possibility for both Part 1 and Part 3, given the spinal-cord-stimulation carry-over literature's own median of 5 hours (Meier et al. 2024) and this project's own finding that a short memory effect cannot be ruled out (decision 262b); a safety and rescue plan needs sign-off from the clinical team, separate from the research design.
4. **The statistical power described above will very likely still leave this a "lead" after one, or even three, visits.** Setting that expectation honestly, up front, avoids over-reading a small, well-matched sample as more than it is.
5. **Sustaining true blinding across a multi-day, multi-week home schedule** needs a reliable caregiver or coordinator; if the patient adjusts their own device day to day, or notices a change in sensation at a different current or rate, the blind may break, which would weaken (though not by itself invalidate) the hold-and-return comparison.
6. **A different subjective sensation at 60 Hz than at 55 Hz** (a change in the tingling or buzzing the patient feels) could itself act as a cue that something changed, introducing an expectation effect on top of, or instead of, any real change in the brain signal.
7. **Whether "Single Threshold Inverse" (sensing-only) mode is compatible with continuing this patient's ordinary therapeutic stimulation unchanged** is a device/firmware question this report cannot answer with confidence; it needs to be checked with Medtronic clinical support or the treating clinician before Part 3's home logging is set up.

---

## Recommendation

In order of value for the time and risk involved:

1. **Run Part 2 (the rate swap) first.** It needs no interruption of ordinary stimulation, takes about an hour (two hours if a second A-B-A cycle is done, which is worth the extra time), and gives a direct, falsifiable answer to whether the stimulator itself is manufacturing the signal in the 24.5-27.5 Hz family — the single biggest open question standing between "this project has a lead" and "this project has a controller." Analyse it the same day if possible, since the recordings and the comparison do not depend on pain reports settling first.
2. **Run Part 1 (the tightly-timed 0 mA block) next, in the same visit if the patient tolerates the combined length, or at a following visit if not.** Even one visit's worth of minutes-not-hours matching is a real improvement over the data already in hand, and it directly answers the microlesion-effect worry raised in the literature above (a block run now, more than a year post-implant, does not carry that confound the way the original one did). Report the result honestly as one well-matched day, not as confirmation.
3. **Set up Part 3 (hold-and-return) as a longer-horizon addition**, contingent on the clinical team confirming the sensing-only re-pointing is safe and workable, and on a caregiver or coordinator being available to hold the blind. This is the piece most likely to need a second and third visit's worth of data (per the AHRQ guide's own recommendation of multiple treatment periods) before it says anything with confidence.
4. **Analyse everything with tools this project already has** — the day-level block bootstrap (decision 183), the current-taken-out machinery (decisions 234, 240-241), and the existing memory-length grid (decision 262b) — rather than building new statistics for a one-off visit.
5. **Report every result at the confidence its sample size supports.** Given the power calculation above, expect this visit (and likely the next two or three) to produce leads to repeat, not settled findings, and say so plainly in whatever is written up afterward, in keeping with this project's own established practice.
