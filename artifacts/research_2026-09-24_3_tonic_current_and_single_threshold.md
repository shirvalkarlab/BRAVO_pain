# Issue 3: What does tonic (continuous) current do to pain, and is a single band-power switch with one threshold too simple a controller?

## Bottom line

- **Continuous stimulation does not leave the recorded signal alone — it can suppress the target rhythm, but it can also manufacture new rhythms locked to the stimulator's own pulse rate.** Both effects are well documented in Parkinson's disease, and both are relevant to the Percept RC's threshold logic, because a manufactured (stimulator-locked) rhythm can imitate a real brain response to stimulation without being one. (HIGH — many independent groups, one device-specific artifact paper)
- **The single-band, one-threshold controller works well in Parkinson's disease because three conditions hold there that have not yet been shown to hold for RCS08's pain signal:** the band falls when you stimulate more (a real negative-feedback loop), the state it tracks changes on the same timescale the device can react on (minutes, from medication or motor fluctuation), and the leading trials screen out contaminated signals before trusting them. None of RCS08's candidate bands has cleared all three bars yet. (HIGH for the Parkinson's evidence; the RCS08 comparison is this project's own measured data, already given as context)
- **Where a single-feature adaptive controller for chronic pain does exist (depression's mood-driven amygdala gamma, one patient), it took months of invasive recording, deliberate stimulation mapping, and a slow-changing feature — not a fast reactive switch — to get there.** The best-performing recent Parkinson's system also needed a signal validated against gold-standard motor exam scores, not spectral shape alone, and it explicitly used a stimulation-created rhythm on purpose, having proven that rhythm tracked the right clinical state. (HIGH for what was published; MEDIUM for how well it generalizes to pain)
- **The literature that most resembles RCS08's own pain problem (thalamic/periventricular-gray recordings during deep brain stimulation for pain) points to a different part of the spectrum than the device can use** — its pain-linked signal was a very slow potential (0.2–0.4 Hz) and a low-frequency band (roughly 5–13 Hz), and the device's own adaptive firmware cannot sense anything below 8 Hz for control. That mismatch, on its own, is a reason a single 8–30 Hz threshold might be the wrong tool no matter how the harmonic problem is solved. (MEDIUM — small, old case series, not RCS08's own leads or pain type)
- **Pain drifting over months, while the device's fastest reaction time is milliseconds to a few minutes and its slowest is a 5-minute ramp, is itself disqualifying for a *fast* single-threshold controller** — but the device still offers two paths that do not require solving that mismatch: run the adaptive engine as a sensing-only monitor (no current change), or keep tonic (continuous) stimulation and let a person retype the setting slowly, on the visit timescale, using an offline biomarker as a guide rather than a controller. (HIGH — read directly from the device's own documented modes)

## Evidence by theme

### Theme 1: What continuous stimulation does to the recorded signal, and how that can manufacture a fake biomarker

**Real suppression of the target rhythm exists and is one of the best-replicated findings in this field.** Kühn et al. (2008, *Journal of Neuroscience* 28(24):6165–6173, PMID 18550758) recorded from the subthalamic nucleus in 11 people with Parkinson's disease and showed that 130 Hz stimulation suppressed the pathological beta rhythm (13–30 Hz) for about 12 seconds after stimulation stopped, and that this suppression tracked improved movement. This is the textbook justification for Percept's control law: "high power means low stimulation" is described in this project's own device notes as an equilibrium correlation, not a control law — the control law raises current when power crosses above the upper switching value, betting that more current will push power back down, the way it does for Parkinsonian beta. **Why it matters for RCS08: the device's whole logic assumes this negative-feedback relationship. If a candidate pain band does not fall when current rises — or worse, rises then falls, satisfying one threshold on both sides of a peak — the control law cannot tell "not enough current" from "too much," which this project's own measurements already show is a live risk (device notes, §2).**

Feldmann, Neumann, Krause, Lofredi, Schneider & Kühn (2021, *European Journal of Neurology* 28(7):2372–2377, DOI 10.1111/ene.14801, PMID 33675144) extended this from the operating room to chronic, ambulatory Percept-type recordings: beta-band suppression tracked how well stimulation was working over weeks, not just seconds. **Limitation: this is still Parkinson's beta, a rhythm already proven to respond to stimulation before anyone tried to control on it. It says nothing about whether an untested pain band will behave the same way.**

**Stimulation can also manufacture a rhythm rather than just suppressing one.** Mathiopoulou, Habets, Feldmann, Busch, Roediger, Behnke, Schneider, Faust & Kühn (2025, *Nature Communications* 16:2956, DOI 10.1038/s41467-025-58132-7) recorded 19 people with Parkinson's disease and found that high-frequency subthalamic stimulation entrained a gamma rhythm at exactly half the stimulation rate ("1:2 entrainment") in 15 of 19 patients — meaning the brain signal was following the stimulator's clock, not its own. The entrained signal's strength rose during movement and tracked better motor performance in the patients who showed it. **This is genuinely double-edged: it shows a stimulator-locked signal can still carry real clinical information, but only because it was validated against an independent movement measure. On RCS08, the equivalent validation — does the harmonic-adjacent band actually predict pain independent of the current that produces the harmonic — has not been shown; the project's own data show the opposite (all bands on RCS08 that "qualify" as tracking pain sit on a stimulator harmonic).**

Sermon, Starr, Denison & Duchet (2024, *Brain Stimulation* 17(2):488–490, DOI 10.1016/j.brs.2024.02.017) modeled why this entrainment happens: half-harmonic ("finely tuned gamma") entrainment is much more likely when a real, pre-existing brain oscillation is already there for the stimulator to grab onto; without a pre-existing rhythm, stimulation can still create gamma activity, but it will not lock to exactly half the pulse rate, and it will not shift when the pulse rate is changed. **This is a theoretical/computational paper (Kuramoto oscillator simulations), not a human recording study — it explains a mechanism but does not itself measure whether RCS08 has a pre-existing rhythm at the harmonic-adjacent frequencies. It does, however, suggest a concrete experiment: change the stimulation rate and see whether the suspect band's peak moves with it (see Testable Predictions, #4).**

**Evoked resonant neural activity (ERNA)** is a related but distinct phenomenon: every single stimulation pulse near the subthalamic nucleus triggers a large, ringing electrical response a few milliseconds later, at an amplitude hundreds of times bigger than ordinary brain signal. Thevathasan, Sinclair, Bulluss & McDermott (2020, *Frontiers in Human Neuroscience* 14:71, DOI 10.3389/fnhum.2020.00071) showed in 14 patients (28 leads) that the contact with the largest such evoked response also gave the best clinical benefit — a genuinely useful, stimulation-triggered signal, used for picking which contact to stimulate through, not for closed-loop switching. Wiest et al. (2025, *Movement Disorders* 40(2):351–356, DOI 10.1002/mds.30063, PMID 39560163) then showed this same evoked signal changes with sleep stage in the same patient. **Why this matters here: even a stimulation-evoked signal, which by definition exists only because of the stimulator, is itself state-dependent (sleep changes it) — a caution against assuming any signal tied to stimulation is therefore stable or artifact-free.**

**Percept-specific recording artifacts are well documented and non-trivial.** Hammer, Kochanski, Starr & Little (2022, *Stereotactic and Functional Neurosurgery* 100(3):168–183, DOI 10.1159/000521431) characterized three sources of contamination in Percept PC recordings: heartbeat (ECG) artifact, especially in certain stimulation configurations; transient artifacts every time a stimulation parameter changes, which distort low frequencies; and other polyphasic artifacts. Their practical recommendation — record with stimulation briefly at 0 mA when trying to establish a clean baseline — is a reminder that "off" and "on" comparisons on this device are not automatically clean. **This is a general Percept-family finding, not RCS08-specific, but it independently supports the concern that a chronic Percept recording can carry non-physiological structure that a naive band-power reader would treat as signal.**

**The core epistemic risk, stated plainly:** if the clinician changes current partly in response to how the patient is doing, and the stimulator's own current also changes the recorded band power (through suppression, entrainment, or artifact), then band power and pain can move together purely because they share a cause — the treatment decision — without the band actually reporting anything about the brain's pain state. This is confounding by a shared cause, and it cannot be resolved by looking within one recording; it needs either a controlled manipulation (change current independent of any decision about pain, e.g., a titration ladder) or a statistical removal of current's own contribution before testing whether a band still relates to pain. RCS08's own analysis already runs both approaches (a manipulated titration ladder and a current-removal statistical model) and, as of this writing, no candidate band survives either check as beating chance out of sample — this is this project's own measured finding, not an external citation, and is treated here as verified context rather than literature.

### Theme 2: What has been tried as single- or few-feature adaptive DBS, and what its limits are

**The device's own algorithm was built and proven on Parkinsonian beta, one feature at a time, in a sequence of increasingly rigorous trials.**

Little et al. (2013, *Annals of Neurology* 74(3):449–457, DOI 10.1002/ana.23951) ran the first human proof-of-concept: in patients with temporarily externalized DBS leads, switching stimulation on and off based on one beta-power threshold gave better motor scores than continuous stimulation while delivering current only part of the time (roughly halving stimulation time in their report). **Limitation: short-duration, small, and unblinded/lightly blinded testing on freshly implanted, externalized leads — not the chronic, ambulatory, fully implanted situation this platform is built for.**

Velisar, Syrkin-Nikolau, Blumenfeld, Trager, Afzal, Prabhakar & Bronte-Stewart (2019, *Brain Stimulation* 12(4):868–876, DOI 10.1016/j.brs.2019.02.020, PMID 30833216) extended this to a **dual threshold**: an upper and lower switching value with a "do nothing" zone in between, which is directly the mode Percept calls Dual Threshold. This design is meant for slower, medication-timescale fluctuations rather than instant on/off. **Why it matters for RCS08: dual threshold is already the option in this device that most resembles "give the biomarker room before reacting," and it is already available in the device's own menu — see Alternatives, below.**

**ADAPT-PD is the definitive, large-scale test of exactly the two algorithms Percept implements.** Stanslaski et al. (2024, *npj Parkinson's Disease* 10:174, DOI 10.1038/s41531-024-00772-5) describe the trial's sensing methodology: single threshold reacts in 250 ms on an alpha-beta band; dual threshold ramps over 2.5 minutes up and 5 minutes down. **A finding worth weighing directly: 9% of enrolled patients were excluded for signal artifact contamination before the algorithm could even be tried**, and clinicians in some cases pre-screened for a clean beta peak before enrolling — both signs that even in Parkinson's disease, on the exact device RCS08 has, a usable single-band signal is not guaranteed and is actively screened for. Bronte-Stewart et al. (2025, *JAMA Neurology*, NCT04547712; accessed via PMC12455485) report the trial's results: comparing 30-day blinded periods of single- versus dual-threshold adaptive stimulation against baseline continuous stimulation in a randomized crossover of people who tolerated both, 91% on dual threshold and 79% on single threshold met a lenient performance goal (no more than 2 hours per day worse "good" time than baseline); there was no significant difference between the two adaptive modes on the primary or secondary outcome; total stimulation energy fell by 13–15% relative to continuous stimulation. The authors themselves caution that comparisons against continuous stimulation were not blinded, so some of the apparent benefit could reflect expectation rather than the algorithm. **Why this matters for RCS08: the single-band controller's real-world track record, even in its home disease, is "roughly as good as continuous stimulation, using somewhat less energy" — not a dramatic win — and it required screening out one in eleven patients for bad signal quality. That is the ceiling of what to expect if RCS08's biggest problems (harmonic contamination, current confound) were solved; it is not evidence that solving them would produce something transformative.**

**Where single-feature control has needed the most help, the answer was not more thresholds but a better-validated, personalized feature.** Oehrn et al. (2024, *Nature Medicine* 30:3345–3356, DOI 10.1038/s41591-024-03196-z, PMID 37649907) ran a blinded, randomized crossover pilot in four men with Parkinson's disease, comparing personalized adaptive stimulation against continuous stimulation. Their key move: they explicitly used **stimulation-entrained gamma** oscillations (the same 1:2 harmonic phenomenon described above) as the control signal, having first shown in each patient that this entrained signal reliably separated their high- and low-symptom states. Adaptive stimulation reduced time spent with motor symptoms and improved patient-reported quality of life compared with continuous stimulation. **Why it matters for RCS08: this is the strongest recent evidence that a device-adjacent, even stimulation-created, signal CAN drive useful adaptive control — but only after the harder step of validating that signal against an independent, gold-standard outcome measure in that specific patient. Nothing in the Percept documentation or RCS08's record yet shows a pain band cleared that bar; RCS08's own harmonic-adjacent bands have not been shown to predict pain out of sample better than chance (this project's own decision 240).**

**Long-term, ambulatory biomarker discovery without needing the clinic is possible, and is the template this platform is already following.** Gilron et al. (2021, *Nature Biotechnology* 39:1078–1085, DOI 10.1038/s41587-021-00897-5, PMID 33941932) streamed brain signals wirelessly from five people with Parkinson's disease for up to 15 months at home, pairing signal with wearable-sensor movement data to find individual biomarkers of too much or too little movement, which were then used to inform adaptive stimulation. **This is on the research-only Summit RC+S, which (per this project's own device notes) allows a weighted, multi-feature, sign-invertible classifier that Percept RC cannot replicate. It is evidence for the discovery *method* — long ambulatory recording plus an independent behavioral label — not for the specific single-band control law RCS08 must eventually fit into.**

**Depression's closed-loop precedent shows a slow-changing, single-feature signal can work, at the cost of an extraordinary discovery process.** Scangos et al. (2021, *Nature Medicine* 27:1696–1700, DOI 10.1038/s41591-021-01480-w, PMID 34608328) report one patient with treatment-resistant depression: days of invasive recording and stimulation mapping identified gamma power in the amygdala as a biomarker of high-symptom states, and stimulating a separate site (ventral capsule/ventral striatum) when that biomarker rose produced rapid, sustained symptom improvement. **This is n=1, with a biomarker discovered over an unusually long and resource-intensive process, and depression's timescale (a mood state lasting hours) is closer to what chronic pain looks like than Parkinsonian beta's second-to-second timescale is. It supports the idea that slow, state-like signals CAN drive single-feature closed loop, but only after a validation effort well beyond a spectral scan.**

**Pain-specific closed-loop work, including this participant's own research group, argues explicitly against a single fast reactive band for chronic pain.** Shirvalkar, Veuthey, Dawes & Chang (2018, *Frontiers in Computational Neuroscience* 12:18, DOI 10.3389/fncom.2018.00018, PMID 29632482) proposed, as a framework paper (not an empirical trial), that because chronic pain has sensory, affective and cognitive components, a useful biomarker likely needs a "state-space" representation built from multiple features rather than one number. Shirvalkar et al. (2023, *Nature Neuroscience* 26(5):1090–1099, DOI 10.1038/s41593-023-01338-z, PMID 37217725) then delivered the empirical case for that view: in four people with chronic neuropathic pain and intracranial electrodes in the anterior cingulate cortex and orbitofrontal cortex, chronic pain state was predicted from **sustained** power changes in the orbitofrontal cortex, using a machine-learning model built from multiple frequency features — and these sustained signals were explicitly different from the brief, transient signals associated with acute, evoked pain. A later trial from the same group and posted as a preprint (Shirvalkar and colleagues, medRxiv 2025.08.11.25333010, "Personalized, closed-loop deep brain stimulation for chronic pain," posted 2025-08-13; already cited as verified in this project's own device documentation) implemented closed-loop pain DBS on the research-only Summit RC+S using a personalized, multi-feature classifier with a switch-able sign, plus a separate sleep classifier to disable stimulation overnight. **Why this matters directly for RCS08: this is the same participant's own research program's best answer to the "how do you close the loop on pain" question, and its answer was "many features, individually fit, on a device that lets a weighted combination in" — not "one 5 Hz band, one threshold." The Percept RC, per this project's own device documentation, structurally cannot do what that trial did.**

**The pain-DBS literature that looked hardest at raw oscillations, rather than machine-learning features, points at a different part of the spectrum than the harmonic-contaminated one on RCS08.** Nandi, Liu, Joint, Stein & Aziz (2002, *Pain* 97(1–2):47–51, PMID 12031778) recorded thalamic field potentials during periventricular gray stimulation in two people with chronic central pain and found that 5–25 Hz stimulation gave maximum pain relief while 50–100 Hz worsened it, and that a very slow potential (0.2–0.4 Hz) fell in amplitude specifically when pain relief occurred. Nandi, Aziz, Carter & Stein (2003, *Pain* 101(1):97–107, PMID 12507704) extended this to eight patients with the same finding (5–35 Hz relieving, 50–100 Hz worsening, the same 0.2–0.4 Hz correlate). Huang, Luo, Green, Aziz & Wang (2016, *Clinical Neurophysiology* 127(7):2573–2580, PMID 27291876) recorded from the sensory thalamus and periventricular/periaqueductal gray in ten patients with neuropathic pain and found theta, alpha and beta power in these structures correlated with pain relief, and that combinations of bands correlated more strongly than any single band alone. Luo, Huang, Xiao, Dai, Nie, Geng, Green, Aziz & Wang (2020, *European Journal of Neuroscience* 51(2):628–640, DOI 10.1111/ejn.14569, PMID 31483893) recorded from the sensory thalamus in twelve neuropathic pain patients and again linked oscillatory dynamics to pain modulation. **Limitations, stated plainly: these are small (n=2 to 12), decades-old-to-recent case series from one research group, using thalamic and periventricular gray targets and externalized-lead recordings taken during active DBS-induced pain relief — not RCS08's own targets, not ambulatory, and not blinded, controlled trials. They should be read as "this is the kind of signal the field has found before," not as proof RCS08 will show the same thing.** Two points transfer directly to RCS08 regardless of target: first, **the stimulation frequency itself mattered as much as any recorded band** in this literature (5–35 Hz helped, 50–100 Hz hurt) — a reminder that "what frequency you stimulate at" and "what frequency you sense in" are two separate design choices, both of which interact with harmonics; second, **the strongest pain-linked signal these groups found (0.2–0.4 Hz) is far below the 8 Hz floor of Percept's adaptive sensing range**, and even their oscillatory bands (theta–low beta, roughly 4–13 Hz) sit mostly below or at the edge of where RCS08's own qualifying bands cluster (21.5–27.5 Hz). If RCS08's real pain signal, if it exists, is a slow potential or a low-frequency oscillation like this older literature suggests, the Percept adaptive engine could not act on it even with a perfectly clean, harmonic-free measurement, because its firmware will not sense below 8 Hz.

### Theme 3: Pain is slow and multidimensional — what that means for "tonic" as the comparator, and for the device's options

**Ambulatory recordings of even a well-validated Parkinsonian signal drift with the time of day, independent of disease state — a general caution about chronic LFP power, not a pain-specific one.** van Rheede, Feldmann, Busch, Fleming, Mathiopoulou, Denison, Sharott & Kühn (2022, *npj Parkinson's Disease*, DOI 10.1038/s41531-022-00350-7, PMID 35804160) found that time of day alone explained 41% ± 9% of the variance in subthalamic beta power across patients (p < 0.001 in every patient), with beta higher during the day and lower at night. **Why this matters for RCS08: this project's own decision 246 independently found the same kind of thing — some RCS08 sensing pairs show a strong daily or weekly rhythm in raw band power unrelated to the pain-linked band family, and pain itself differs between weekday and weekend. The van Rheede paper shows this is not a fluke of RCS08's data or of pain specifically; ambulatory chronic LFP power in general carries slow rhythms that have nothing to do with the symptom being tracked, and any single-band threshold has to survive that background drift, not just the harmonic problem.**

**Continuous ("tonic") stimulation is not sacred even within pain neuromodulation — the field already treats waveform pattern as a free variable, though not yet for pain DBS specifically.** Deer et al. (2018, *Neuromodulation* 21(1):56–66, DOI not independently confirmed; PMID 28961366 — the SUNBURST trial) randomized 100 people who had already responded to tonic spinal cord stimulation to 12-week crossover periods of tonic versus "burst" (a patterned, non-continuous waveform) stimulation for chronic pain. Burst was non-inferior to tonic on the trial's primary pain-relief measure and patients strongly preferred it (70.8% preferred burst versus 18.8% preferred tonic at the primary endpoint, sustained to one year at 68.2% versus 23.9%). **Limitation: this is spinal cord stimulation, a different anatomical target and mechanism from DBS, and it compares two continuous stimulation patterns, not a fast reactive on/off switch. It is cited here only to make the narrower point the PI raised: "continuous" is a specific choice among several, tested and sometimes beaten, in other parts of the neuromodulation field, and the assumption that continuous is the right baseline to compare adaptive control against deserves the same scrutiny in DBS.**

**DBS itself already loses efficacy over time in some patients (habituation), and clinics already manage this without any closed-loop biomarker at all.** Peters & Tisch (2021, *Frontiers in Neurology* 12:696950, DOI 10.3389/fneur.2021.696950) review habituation to DBS in tremor: benefit fades in an estimated 4–42% of the effect across long-term follow-up studies (evidence quality mixed), and clinics already use **scheduled cycling** (alternating on/off blocks, switching programs weekly, or nightly stimulation holidays) as a practical, low-tech response, independent of any sensed signal. **Why it matters for RCS08: this is a real, already-practiced alternative to both "always-on tonic" and "biomarker-driven adaptive" — a fixed schedule, chosen by the clinician, that does not require solving the harmonic or confound problem at all. Whether RCS08's specific Percept configuration exposes a cycling parameter separately from its Parkinson's adaptive mode was not checked in this project's documents and should be confirmed with the clinical team before assuming it is available; this is general DBS-field knowledge, not a project-specific fact.**

## Alternatives the device allows, and ones it does not

**Allowed today, on this device, in this participant's configuration:**

1. **Dual Threshold instead of Single Threshold.** Already built into Percept and directly informed by the Velisar (2019) design: an upper and lower switching value with a "do nothing" zone between them, and much slower ramps (2.5 minutes up, 5 minutes down) than Single Threshold's 250 milliseconds. This is the device's own built-in answer to "react more slowly," though its slowest built-in ramp (5 minutes) is still far faster than the month-scale drift this project has measured in RCS08's pain.
2. **Choosing a sensing band away from the stimulator's own harmonics, within the 8–30 Hz firmware limit.** This project's own work has already identified which centres are clear of the folded harmonic at the current stimulation rate (55 Hz folds a harmonic into 25 and 30 Hz, leaving roughly 24.5 Hz as the nearest clean candidate in the family being watched). This is a real, immediately available lever and does not require any device change — only a different choice of which 5 Hz slice to sense.
3. **Running the adaptive engine as a monitor only, with "Single Threshold Inverse."** The device documentation is explicit that this mode does not drive stimulation — it senses and can log threshold crossings without ever changing current. This is a legitimate way to accumulate more of the device's own native power readings on a candidate band (this project's own device notes show under 2% of RCS08's record currently has a native device-side power reading; almost everything is inferred from a separately calibrated model) before ever trusting a crossing to move current.
4. **A human-mediated "slow adaptive" workflow**: keep tonic stimulation, and have the clinician retype a new setting at each visit based on a trend computed offline from days to weeks of chronic data. This uses no special device feature beyond ordinary reprogramming, but it directly matches the prompt's idea of adjusting a setpoint on a slow timescale, using the device only to deliver a steady current between visits.
5. **Schedule-based cycling of continuous stimulation** (fixed on/off blocks, or programs alternated on a calendar), independent of any sensed signal, is standard practice for managing habituation elsewhere in DBS (Peters & Tisch, 2021). Whether it is available as a separate parameter in RCS08's specific Parkinson's-adaptive configuration was not verified in this project's documents.

**Not allowed, on this device, as documented:**

1. **Any multi-band or ratio feature as the live control signal.** The firmware takes exactly one band's power in and produces one or two switching values out (this project's own device documentation, §1). A ratio of two bands, or any weighted combination, can be computed offline to help a clinician choose which single band and threshold to program, but it can never itself be the thing the tablet enacts on Percept RC — that capability exists only on the research-only Summit RC+S (used in Gilron et al. 2021 and in this participant's own group's closed-loop pain trial).
2. **Sensing outside 8–30 Hz for adaptive control.** This firmware limit rules out ever using the very slow (0.2–0.4 Hz) potential or the low-frequency theta/alpha band that the classical thalamic/periventricular-gray pain literature (Nandi et al. 2002, 2003; Huang et al. 2016; Luo et al. 2020) found most informative, even if an equivalent signal turned out to be RCS08's best offline biomarker.
3. **Any control cadence between the device's fastest (100 ms to 5 minutes) and a clinician's next visit.** Nothing in Percept's adaptive firmware lets the device itself change a setpoint automatically over hours to days; that gap can only be filled by a person (alternative 4, above).
4. **Sign inversion or a weighted linear combination of features**, as used on the Summit RC+S in this participant's own group's earlier closed-loop pain trial. This project's own device documentation states this explicitly and warns that any design assuming it is designing for the wrong device.
5. **Patient- or event-triggered bolus changes** as part of an adaptive group. Not modeled anywhere in this platform and not part of the Parkinson's adaptive mode this participant is configured in.

## Testable predictions in RCS08's data

Each of these is falsifiable against data this project already has, or could collect with the existing pipeline, without programming anything to the device.

1. **If a candidate band is a real state signal and not stimulator entrainment, it should beat its own shuffled-data null out of sample even after current's own contribution is statistically removed.** This project's own held-out-in-time, current-adjusted check (decision 240/241) already exists; as of this writing, no candidate band on either side clears its own null (best case 0.701 against a 95th-percentile null of 0.713). Prediction: until a band clears this bar, no single-threshold design should be considered validated, regardless of which band or threshold is chosen.
2. **If a band is truly stimulator-entrained rather than reporting the brain's own pain state, holding current exactly fixed while pain is independently perturbed should decouple the two.** The exploratory titration ladder's blind ON/OFF/ON holds at a fixed current (already designed, decision 230/237, not yet run live) is exactly this test. Prediction: a truly entrained-only band will show no rating change across the hold blocks that current alone cannot explain; a real pain-tracking band will.
3. **If RCS08's real pain-linked signal sits below 8 Hz or is a very slow potential, as the classical thalamic/PVG literature suggests, it should be visible in the existing offline exploration grid (which already spans 2.5–99.5 Hz) even though the device could never act on it.** Prediction: if a stronger, cleaner pain relationship appears below 8 Hz than anywhere in the 8–30 Hz adaptive range, that alone is evidence the single-band 8–30 Hz controller is the wrong tool for RCS08, independent of the harmonic problem.
4. **If the harmonic, not a real brain rhythm, explains why bands near 25–27.5 Hz look pain-related, then changing the stimulation rate from 55 Hz to 60 Hz (which, per this project's own device notes, folds its harmonic to a different, non-overlapping place) while holding current and contacts fixed should shift or collapse the effect.** This controlled swap has not yet been run on RCS08 and is a concrete, low-risk next experiment.
5. **If pain is a slow (multi-hour to day-scale) process, the useful autocorrelation timescale of any real pain-linked band, measured the same way the purged time-blocked cross-validation already estimates it (decision 240), should be long — much longer than the device's fastest reaction windows (100 ms to 5 minutes).** Prediction: measuring and reporting this timescale explicitly, for any future candidate band, will show whether a fast reactive threshold could ever track it in principle, separate from whether the band is otherwise clean.
6. **Given that even Parkinson's disease excludes about 1 in 11 patients from single/dual-threshold adaptive DBS for signal artifact (Stanslaski et al. 2024), RCS08's own device-side ceiling/artifact check (already built, this project's decision 33/52) should be run and reported as a number** — an artifact or saturation rate for the specific flanking-contact pairs the device's own polarity rule permits (L 1-3+, R 0-3+) — before any threshold on those contacts is considered trustworthy.

## Verdict

A single band, one threshold, is a reasonable controller when three conditions hold together: **(a)** the band has been shown to respond causally and consistently in one direction to more current, so raising current when the band crosses the switching value actually pushes it back down (the negative-feedback assumption this device's own control law makes, stated explicitly in this project's device documentation); **(b)** the state the band is meant to track changes on a timescale the device's reaction windows can resolve — tens of seconds to a few minutes for Single Threshold, minutes for Dual Threshold — not months; **(c)** the band's relationship to the signal it is supposed to track has been checked for stimulator artifact and entrainment and, ideally, validated against an independent, gold-standard measure of the state, the way Oehrn et al. (2024) validated their entrained-gamma feature against motor exam scores before trusting it.

Parkinsonian beta, as used in Little (2013), Velisar (2019), and ADAPT-PD (Stanslaski et al. 2024; Bronte-Stewart et al. 2025), meets all three reasonably well, and even there the real-world result is modest (roughly matching continuous stimulation's benefit while using somewhat less energy, in an unblinded comparison, with about 9% of patients excluded for bad signal). None of the three conditions has yet been shown to hold for any RCS08 pain candidate: **(a)** is unresolved and, per this project's own device documentation, actively broken for at least one band (rises then falls with current, defeating the two-point logic); **(b)** is very likely false, given the month-scale drift already documented at a fixed setting in this participant, against the device's millisecond-to-minutes reaction windows; and **(c)** is currently failing — every RCS08 band that "qualifies" as pain-tracking sits on a stimulator harmonic, and no band beats its own out-of-sample null once current is accounted for.

**What RCS08's data would need to show, specifically, before a single-band-one-threshold configuration should be trusted as a controller:**

1. A band that beats its own held-out-in-time, current-adjusted null (the check already built, decision 240/241) — none does yet.
2. A demonstrated causal response of that band to current, from a controlled, blind manipulation at fixed current (the exploratory ladder's ON/OFF/ON holds, decision 230/237) — not yet run live.
3. A band whose own natural timescale of change is compatible with the device's fastest usable window — given the drift already measured over months at one fixed setting, this may not exist for any band the firmware can sense, in which case the honest and available use of this device today is either as a slow, human-supervised setpoint (retyped at each visit from an offline trend) or as a sensing-only monitor, not as an autonomous fast controller.

The PI's intuition — "this single band-power limit is certainly an oversimplification of the device" — is well supported by the literature on both sides of the analogy: the Parkinson's field's own best recent results needed either a validated, artifact-screened single feature (ADAPT-PD, at a scale RCS08's n=1 record cannot yet claim) or a personalized, gold-standard-validated feature and often more than one signal (Oehrn 2024; Gilron 2021); the pain and depression closed-loop precedents that exist at all (Shirvalkar 2023; Scangos 2021; the 2025 pain DBS trial) use multiple features or a slow, carefully validated single one, on a device that allows what Percept RC does not.
