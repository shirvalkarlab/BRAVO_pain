## Bottom line

- **The general phenomenon is real and repeatedly demonstrated, just not yet in pain DBS.** In Parkinson's DBS, TMS, tACS, depression DBS/SEEG, and epilepsy responsive neurostimulation (RNS), the same stimulation pulse or pattern produces different, sometimes opposite, physiological or clinical effects depending on the oscillatory phase, oscillatory power, or broader "network state" present when it is delivered (Cagnan et al. 2017, *Brain*; Holt et al. 2019, *J Neurosci*; Zrenner et al. 2018, *Brain Stimul*; Alagapan et al. 2016, *PLoS Biol*; Scangos et al. 2021, *Nat Med*; Chiang et al. 2021 & 2025). This makes the PI's hypothesis biologically plausible and consistent with a mature, cross-disorder literature — it is not a special pleading for RCS08's own drift-free E1 model.
- **The clearest and most load-bearing analogy for RCS08 is not Parkinson's beta — it is depression and OCD DBS with chronic sensing**, because those studies used the same kind of ambulatory, multi-day, patient-implanted electrophysiology RCS08 has, and they explicitly found that identical stimulation produced state- and context-dependent behavioral responses (Scangos et al. 2021, *Nat Med*), and that a neural feature's *predictive/pathological* character (not its mean level) tracked clinical state and treatment response over months (Provenza et al. 2024, *Nat Med*, OCD; Kirkby et al. 2018, *Cell*, mood).
- **Pain DBS's own older literature already reports a version of this without calling it "state dependence": tolerance.** Kumar and colleagues' 15-year and 68-patient PVG/thalamic series reported an early tolerance-driven fall-off in relief within about the first two years, stabilizing thereafter, "regardless of site of implant" (Kumar, Toth & Nath 1997, *Neurosurgery*; Kumar, Wyant & Nath 1990, *Neurosurgery*; Levy, Lamb & Adams 1987, *Neurosurgery*). Tolerance and "response depends on state" are not identical claims but they are cousins: both say a fixed input does not produce a fixed output over time, and neither has ever been mechanistically separated from the other in the pain-DBS literature.
- **Several mundane explanations produce the identical statistical signature** (a fixed setting whose apparent benefit changes across blocks of time) without any change in brain responsiveness at all: regression to the mean, a placebo/expectation trajectory that itself decays over weeks (a well-documented feature of neurostimulation placebo arms), weekday/weekend mood and activity cycles (which RCS08's own record already shows: pain runs higher at weekends, decision 246), and simple non-independence of repeated ratings from the same person in the same short window. These have to be ruled out, or explicitly modeled alongside, before "network state" is invoked.
- **Testing this rigorously in RCS08's data is possible but sample-limited.** The device gives one thing state-dependence hypotheses need — band power in the minutes before a stimulation change — but the number of *stimulation changes* (as opposed to pain ratings) is small (order of tens over the study, per decision 213/239's run counts), so a state-by-current interaction test has much less power than the correlational analyses already run. The realistic first step is not a full interaction model; it is a pre-registered, focused test on the titration-ladder data already being collected (decisions 230, 236, 237, 239), because those are the only records with dense, repeated exposures to the same current at different times.

## Evidence, grouped by theme

### 1. DBS: phase- and state-dependent modulation of pathological oscillations (Parkinson's disease)

**Cagnan H, Pedrosa D, Little S, et al. "Stimulating at the right time: phase-specific deep brain stimulation." *Brain*. 2017;140(1):132–145. doi:10.1093/brain/aww286. PMID: 28007997.**
Population: patients with Parkinson's disease and essential tremor with externalized DBS leads (STN and VIM), tested acutely, days after implant, while the wires were temporarily exposed for research recording. Method: single stimulation pulses were timed to specific phases of the ongoing tremor-frequency or beta-frequency oscillation and the resulting change in oscillation amplitude was measured over the following cycles. Finding: the *same pulse*, delivered at different phases of the same underlying oscillation, either suppressed or amplified the oscillation — the sign of the response flipped depending only on timing relative to ongoing brain activity, not on stimulation amplitude. Limitation: acute, externalized-lead recordings over minutes, not chronic implanted therapy over months; oscillation is tremor/beta in a movement disorder, not the 21.5–27.5 Hz pain-associated bands on RCS08's L 1-3+. Relevance: this is the cleanest possible existence proof that identical stimulation energy delivered at two different instants of ongoing brain activity produces opposite outcomes — the mechanistic backbone of the PI's hypothesis, even though the target and disease differ completely.

**Holt AB, Kormann E, Gulberti A, et al. "Phase-Dependent Suppression of Beta Oscillations in Parkinson's Disease Patients." *J Neurosci*. 2019;39(6):1119–1134. doi:10.1523/JNEUROSCI.1913-18.2018. PMID: 30552179.**
Population: 8 Parkinson's patients (2 excluded), intraoperative STN recordings during electrode implantation. Method: biphasic pulses at beta frequency delivered at one of eight phase bins relative to the ongoing beta cycle; effect on beta amplitude tracked over consecutive cycles. Finding: stimulation at a patient-specific "suppressing phase" progressively reduced beta amplitude — 21.8% after two pulses, 46.8% after five, reaching significance beyond natural variability after the third pulse (p = 0.0031); the *same* stimulation at the "amplifying phase" increased beta. Limitation: intraoperative, single session, beta-band Parkinsonian mechanism. Relevance: mechanistically extends Cagnan (above): repeated pulses at a fixed relationship to state compound the effect, meaning consistency of *when* you stimulate relative to state matters as much as *what* you deliver — directly analogous to asking whether RCS08's fixed continuous current interacts differently with the brain depending on the ongoing 21.5–27.5 Hz activity at delivery.

**Little S, Pogosyan A, Neal S, et al. "Adaptive deep brain stimulation in advanced Parkinson disease." *Ann Neurol*. 2013;74(3):449–457. doi:10.1002/ana.23951. PMID: 23852650.**
Population: 8 patients with advanced Parkinson's disease, externalized leads. Method: closed-loop ("adaptive") DBS that switched stimulation on only when STN beta power crossed an amplitude threshold, compared against continuous stimulation at matched mean current. Finding: adaptive stimulation, triggered by state (beta threshold crossing) rather than delivered continuously, improved motor scores by about 66% versus continuous DBS's roughly 50% while using markedly less total stimulation. Limitation: acute (minutes to an hour), externalized-lead study, not a chronic implanted comparison; beta threshold used a single band and a single fixed cutoff, the same architecture pattern this project's own closed-loop candidate would use. Relevance: this is the direct precedent for a single-band, single-threshold controller, and its success came specifically *because* it exploited state dependence (beta level) rather than ignoring it — the flip side of the concern in Issue 1: if a controller does not know which underlying state the band is currently indexing, it can neither reproduce this benefit nor avoid its failure modes (see Implications section).

**Tinkhauser G, Pogosyan A, Little S, et al. "The modulatory effect of adaptive deep brain stimulation on beta bursts in Parkinson's disease." *Brain*. 2017;140(4):1053–1067. doi:10.1093/brain/awx010. PMID: 28334851.**
Population: Parkinson's patients with externalized STN leads (overlapping cohort with Little 2013). Method: characterized beta activity not as a continuous level but as discrete "bursts" of varying duration and amplitude, and examined how adaptive versus continuous stimulation changed the burst-duration distribution. Finding: pathologically long beta bursts (correlated with worse motor impairment) were selectively shortened by adaptive stimulation, while conventional continuous stimulation did not preferentially target them; burst duration, not mean beta power, carried the clinically relevant information. Limitation: same acute externalized-lead constraint; Parkinson's beta, not pain-associated bands. Relevance: directly warns against the specific architecture RCS08's Biomarkers pipeline uses today (band power averaged over a fixed window, one threshold): if the state that matters is a *temporal pattern* (burst duration, or here, hypothetically, the instant of stimulation onset relative to an oscillatory episode) rather than a *level*, then two recordings with identical average power in a window can represent two different underlying states with different relationships to pain — a mechanism that could produce exactly the "same current, different outcome" pattern the PI describes.

### 2. TMS and tACS: pre-stimulus brain state predicts response variability

**Iscan Z, Nazarova M, Fedele T, Blagovechtchenski E, Nikulin VV. "Pre-stimulus alpha oscillations and inter-subject variability of motor evoked potentials in single- and paired-pulse TMS paradigms." *Front Hum Neurosci*. 2016;10:504. doi:10.3389/fnhum.2016.00504.**
Population: 17 healthy adults. Method: concurrent EEG-EMG during single-pulse and paired-pulse (intracortical facilitation/inhibition) TMS to motor cortex, examining pre-stimulus sensorimotor alpha power and its trial-to-trial variability against motor-evoked-potential (MEP) size. Finding: mean pre-stimulus alpha power did not predict MEP amplitude, but the *variability* of high-alpha power (10–12 Hz) predicted the variability of MEP amplitude specifically during the intracortical-facilitation paradigm (p<0.05). Limitation: healthy volunteers, single motor-cortex outcome, modest sample. Relevance: an important nuance for RCS08 — it is not always the average level of a rhythm that predicts response to a perturbation, sometimes it is how unstable that rhythm is at the moment of the perturbation. This argues for testing band-power *variance* in the pre-change window, not only its mean, as a state predictor.

**Zrenner C, Desideri D, Belardinelli P, Ziemann U. "Real-time EEG-defined excitability states determine efficacy of TMS-induced plasticity in human motor cortex." *Brain Stimul*. 2018;11(2):374–389. doi:10.1016/j.brs.2017.11.016. PMID: 29191438.**
Population: healthy adults undergoing paired-associative-stimulation plasticity protocols. Method: real-time EEG-triggered TMS delivered only when sensorimotor mu-alpha was at a high-power ("desynchronized"/"synchronized") state, comparing plasticity outcomes with state-blind delivery. Finding: triggering the identical plasticity protocol on one brain state versus another produced opposite plasticity outcomes (potentiation versus no effect/depression) at the group level. Limitation: acute single-session healthy-volunteer plasticity paradigm, not a chronic implanted disease population. Relevance: a rigorously controlled demonstration, in humans, that *timing an identical intervention to brain state* can be the difference between a treatment "working" and "not working" — the strongest single piece of evidence in this literature for the general shape of the PI's hypothesis, albeit in a completely different modality (TMS plasticity, not tonic DBS current).

**Schaworonkow N, Triesch J, Ziemann U, Zrenner C. "EEG-triggered TMS reveals stronger brain state-dependent modulation of motor evoked potentials at weaker stimulation intensities." *Brain Stimul*. 2019;12(1):110–118. doi:10.1016/j.brs.2018.09.009. PMID: 30268710.**
Population: healthy adults. Method: single-pulse TMS triggered by real-time EEG at defined mu-alpha phase/power states, at multiple stimulation intensities. Finding: the state-dependent modulation of MEP amplitude was strongest at *weaker* stimulation intensities and essentially disappeared at supra-threshold intensities — i.e., state dependence is not a fixed property of the system, it interacts with how strong the perturbation is. Limitation: healthy motor cortex, acute. Relevance: important caveat for RCS08 — if state dependence exists here, it may be strongest at the currents nearest threshold for an effect, and weak or undetectable at either very low or very high currents; this argues against expecting a uniform state effect across the whole current-response curve, and toward looking for it near a candidate "elbow" in the dose-response curve rather than across the full range.

**Ruhnau P, Neuling T, Fuscá M, Herrmann CS, Demarchi G, Weisz N. "Eyes wide shut: Transcranial alternating current stimulation drives alpha rhythm in a state dependent manner." *Sci Rep*. 2016;6:27138. doi:10.1038/srep27138. PMID: 27252047.**
Population: healthy adults. Method: 10 Hz tACS delivered with eyes open versus eyes closed (a simple, robust manipulation of ongoing alpha state), measuring after-effects on endogenous alpha power. Finding: tACS entrainment after-effects were clear with eyes open (low baseline alpha) but much weaker with eyes closed (high baseline alpha) — the pre-existing state of the targeted rhythm determined whether the same stimulation altered it at all. Limitation: alpha/visual system, non-invasive scalp stimulation, healthy volunteers — a large step removed from a bilateral DBS current in a chronic-pain patient. Relevance: a simple, well-replicated demonstration that a fixed periodic stimulus's ability to entrain or move a brain rhythm depends on the pre-stimulation level of that same rhythm; conceptually parallel to asking whether the 55 Hz current's effect on the 21.5–27.5 Hz bands on RCS08's left lead depends on where those bands were sitting when the current was applied.

**Alagapan S, Schmidt SL, Lefebvre J, Hadar E, Shin HW, Fröhlich F. "Modulation of Cortical Oscillations by Low-Frequency Direct Cortical Stimulation Is State-Dependent." *PLoS Biol*. 2016;14(8):e1002424. doi:10.1371/journal.pbio.1002424. PMID: 27023427.**
Population: epilepsy patients undergoing intracranial monitoring (direct human cortical recording, closest anatomically to RCS08's setup among the non-DBS studies here). Method: low-frequency direct electrical stimulation delivered to cortex at different phases of the ongoing slow oscillation, tracking entrainment of cortical rhythms. Finding: the same stimulation waveform either entrained (locked to) or failed to entrain the underlying cortical oscillation depending on its phase relationship at onset, and this state dependence was reproduced in a companion computational model, giving a mechanistic account (oscillator phase-response curves) rather than only a correlational one. Limitation: cortical surface/depth electrodes, not deep basal ganglia/limbic DBS; acute stimulation blocks, not months of continuous therapeutic current. Relevance: the most mechanistically explicit paper in this list — it supplies a *model*, not just an observation, for why a fixed electrical stimulus interacts differently with a fluctuating oscillator depending on when in its cycle stimulation starts, which is the literal mathematical structure of the PI's hypothesis applied to the 21.5–27.5 Hz bands.

**Bradley C, Nydam AS, Dux PE, Mattingley JB. "State-dependent effects of neural stimulation on brain function and cognition." *Nat Rev Neurosci*. 2022;23(8):459–475. doi:10.1038/s41583-022-00598-1. PMID: 35577959.**
This is a review, not a primary study: it synthesizes TMS and transcranial electrical stimulation evidence across arousal state (sleep, anesthesia), attention, and working-memory manipulations, concluding that stimulation effects are consistently and substantially modulated by the concurrent physiological or cognitive state, across modalities and target regions, with the caveat that most of the underlying primary literature is in healthy volunteers, acute single sessions, and non-invasive (lower spatial/temporal precision) stimulation. Relevance for RCS08: establishes that "state dependence" is not a niche finding restricted to phase-locked motor-cortex TMS; it is now regarded as a general property of stimulated brains across arousal states and cognitive states, which raises the prior that it also applies to tonic DBS current effects on limbic/sensory circuits, while cautioning that translating this to a chronically implanted, non-blinded, single-patient, months-long DBS record is a real inferential leap, not a direct replication.

### 3. Depression and OCD DBS with chronic ambulatory sensing — the closest structural analogue to RCS08

**Kirkby LA, Luongo FJ, Lee MB, et al. "An Amygdala-Hippocampus Subnetwork that Encodes Variation in Human Mood." *Cell*. 2018;175(6):1688–1700.e14. doi:10.1016/j.cell.2018.10.005. PMID: 30415834.**
Population: 21 patients with intracranial electrodes (implanted for epilepsy monitoring, not DBS) with self-reported mood tracked over multiple days. Method: multi-site intracranial recording combined with machine learning to find network signatures (band coherence between structures) that co-varied with day-to-day self-reported mood. Finding: in 13 of 21 subjects, amygdala-hippocampus beta-band (13–30 Hz) coherence tracked mood variation, and the strength of this relationship differed by trait anxiety; the specific frequency band that carried the state signal was in the same broad range (13–30 Hz) as RCS08's monitored 8.5–29.5 Hz bands, though a different structure pair and a different symptom. Limitation: correlational, no stimulation delivered in this dataset — it establishes that beta-band network coherence tracks mood state day to day, but not that stimulating in the presence of one coherence state versus another changes outcome differently. Relevance: shows that a beta-range signal's *relationship to a symptom* can itself be a property of an underlying, slowly-drifting network state (here indexed by trait anxiety) rather than a fixed, universal mapping — directly analogous to the concern that RCS08's 21.5–27.5 Hz-to-pain relationship might only hold under certain conditions (e.g., only at certain currents, certain times) rather than universally.

**Scangos KW, Makhoul GS, Sugrue LP, Chang EF, Krystal AD. "State-dependent responses to intracranial brain stimulation in a patient with depression." *Nat Med*. 2021;27(2):229–231. doi:10.1038/s41591-020-01175-8. PMID: 33462446.**
Population: single patient (n=1) with severe treatment-resistant depression, multi-site sEEG implant. Method: systematic acute stimulation testing across sites (including ventral capsule/ventral striatum) and parameters, repeated on different days/contexts, scoring the elicited emotional/behavioral response each time. Finding: identical stimulation parameters delivered to the identical contact on different occasions produced qualitatively different, context- and state-dependent emotional responses — the paper's own framing is that responses were "reproducible" in kind but modulated by concurrent behavioral/affective context, i.e., not a fixed stimulus-response mapping. Limitation: n=1, acute test stimulation (seconds to minutes per trial) during an inpatient stay, not months of continuous ambulatory DBS current — a much shorter timescale than RCS08's blocks (weeks). Relevance: this is the single closest published precedent to what the PI is describing: the same electrical input to the same limbic-adjacent target produced a different behavioral effect depending on the state the brain was in when it was delivered, documented with real intracranial recordings in a chronic-disease human implant, the same broad category of evidence RCS08 generates.

**Scangos KW, Khambhati AN, Daly PM, et al. "Closed-loop neuromodulation in an individual with treatment-resistant depression." *Nat Med*. 2021;27(10):1696–1700. doi:10.1038/s41591-021-01480-w. PMID: 34608328.**
Population: single patient (n=1), severe treatment-resistant depression, 10-day intracranial mapping followed by chronic NeuroPace RNS implant. Method: identified bilateral amygdala gamma power as a biomarker discriminating high- versus low-symptom-severity states (accuracy ≈0.77, AUC ≈0.82), then delivered closed-loop stimulation to the ventral capsule/ventral striatum triggered by that state, rather than continuously. Finding: closed-loop, state-triggered stimulation produced a rapid drop in depression severity (MADRS 33→14 over 12 days) sustained over follow-up, with amygdala gamma power correlating with depression severity (r²=0.79, p=4.6×10⁻⁵). Limitation: single case, no blinded comparison to continuous stimulation at the same total dose, so it cannot separate "state-triggering helped" from "more or better-targeted stimulation helped." Relevance: the clearest chronic-implant demonstration that a state-conditioned trigger (as opposed to a fixed continuous setting) can produce durable clinical benefit — the therapeutic argument for reading a band's *state*, not only its threshold crossing.

**Provenza NR, Reddy S, Allam AK, et al. "Disruption of neural periodicity predicts clinical response after deep brain stimulation for obsessive-compulsive disorder." *Nat Med*. 2024;30(10):3004–3014. doi:10.1038/s41591-024-03125-0. PMID: 38997607.**
Population: 12 patients with treatment-resistant OCD receiving DBS with chronic ventral striatal sensing (5-patient discovery cohort, 7-patient held-out replication cohort) — same broad device class and same style of long-term ambulatory sensing as RCS08. Method: characterized ventral striatal neural activity's temporal structure (its circadian periodicity/predictability), not just its average level, across weeks-to-months of DBS. Finding: strong circadian periodicity/high predictability of the biomarker was a signature of the severely symptomatic pre-DBS state; DBS responders showed a substantial *reduction* in that periodicity/predictability post-DBS, while non-responders retained it — meaning the informative signal was a property of the biomarker's *temporal dynamics over weeks*, not a static threshold. Limitation: OCD, ventral striatum, not pain or the thalamic/PAG-type targets typically used in pain DBS; RCS08's own implant target is not documented in this project's records. Relevance: directly on point for the "time is not modeled" premise this project has adopted (decisions 193–196) — this paper shows that in a comparable chronic DBS/sensing population, treating the biomarker's evolution over weeks as informative (rather than assuming a fixed relationship) was the discovery that predicted clinical response, i.e., an explicit counter-example to the idea that ignoring slow temporal structure is safe.

### 4. Chronic-pain DBS's own literature already reports "the same current stopped doing the same thing" — as tolerance

**Levy RM, Lamb S, Adams JE. "Treatment of chronic pain by deep brain stimulation: long term follow-up and review of the literature." *Neurosurgery*. 1987;21(6):885–893. doi:10.1227/00006123-198712000-00017. PMID: 3325851.**
Population: 141 patients across PAG/PVG, sensory thalamus, and internal capsule targets, mean follow-up ~80 months. Finding: lasting relief was obtained in 47% of patients with deafferentation pain and 60% with nociceptive pain — already, in the earliest large pain-DBS series, roughly half the implants that initially worked stopped working over long-term follow-up, without any change in the electrode or the programmed settings being the stated cause. Limitation: retrospective, heterogeneous targets and etiologies, no systematic neural recording to say *why* efficacy fell — tolerance is inferred from outcome, not measured mechanistically. Relevance: establishes the base rate for "loss of effect at a fixed setting" in pain DBS long before closed-loop sensing existed; it is the outcome-level analog of the RCS08 finding that a fixed 1.6/1.2 mA setting's pain objective fell across three blocks of time.

**Kumar K, Wyant GM, Nath R. "Deep brain stimulation for control of intractable pain in humans, present and future: a ten-year follow-up." *Neurosurgery*. 1990;26(5):774–781. doi:10.1097/00006123-199005000-00007. PMID: 2352595.**
Finding (as characterized in the DBS-for-pain review literature that cites this paper): long-term pain control was achieved in the majority of patients who proceeded to full implant, but there was a fall-off in pain control over roughly the first two years attributed to "idiopathic tolerance," with results stabilizing thereafter, regardless of target site — i.e., a time-varying response to a fixed intervention that the original authors already attributed to something changing in the patient/tissue relationship rather than in the device. Limitation: this row is built from a secondary characterization found in review sources rather than a full read of the primary Kumar 1990 abstract text; the paper's own hypothesis for the mechanism (opioid receptor changes, gliosis around the electrode, disease progression) was not separable in that era's data, and none of it distinguishes "network-state dependence" from other explanations. Relevance: an early, explicit statement in the pain-DBS literature that identical stimulation parameters produced diminishing effect over a period of stable time, which is one operational definition of "the same current does not always do the same thing" — the PI's hypothesis reframes this old observation (tolerance, previously blamed on tissue/receptor changes) as possibly reflecting brain-state or network dynamics instead.

**Kumar K, Toth C, Nath RK. "Deep brain stimulation for intractable pain: a 15-year experience." *Neurosurgery*. 1997;40(4):736–747. doi:10.1097/00006123-199704000-00015. PMID: 9092847.**
Population: 68 patients with chronic pain (mostly failed back surgery syndrome) treated with PVG/thalamic/internal capsule DBS. Finding: 53 of 68 patients (77%) proceeded to full implantation after successful trial stimulation; of those, 42 of 53 (79%) continued to have adequate relief at follow-up, for an overall 42/68 (62%) long-term success rate — meaning roughly 1 in 5 patients who initially had a good enough trial response to be implanted subsequently lost adequate relief at the same target and, implicitly, similar settings. Limitation: retrospective case series, decades old, no LFP recording to test *why* relief was lost in that fifth of patients. Relevance: reinforces that loss of efficacy at a previously effective, unchanged (or minimally re-titrated) setting is a recurring, non-rare event in pain DBS's own history — a real clinical phenomenon the PI's hypothesis is trying to explain mechanistically rather than write off as noise.

### 5. Responsive neurostimulation for epilepsy — state-dependence measured directly from chronic device logs (methodologically closest to what RCS08's data would need)

**Chiang S, Khambhati AN, Wang ET, Vannucci M, Chang EF, Rao VR. "Evidence of state-dependence in the effectiveness of responsive neurostimulation for seizure modulation." *Brain Stimul*. 2021;14(2):366–375. doi:10.1016/j.brs.2021.01.023. PMID: 33556620.**
Population: patients implanted with the NeuroPace RNS responsive neurostimulator for drug-resistant epilepsy, using years of continuously logged device data (detections, stimulations, and the device's own long-term epileptiform-activity trend, "ECoG-based cyclical patterns"). Method: modeled whether the same responsive stimulation event was followed by a reduction in subsequent seizure-related activity differently depending on the *state* (position in a multi-day/multi-week cyclical pattern of brain activity) at the time stimulation was delivered. Finding: stimulation effectiveness (its measurable effect on subsequent detected epileptiform activity) varied systematically with the state the device's own long-term trend indicated at delivery time — evidence directly analogous to the PI's proposal, mined from routine chronic-device data rather than a special experiment. Limitation: epilepsy, not pain; "effectiveness" was inferred from detection-rate changes on the device's own log, not from an independent gold-standard outcome; retrospective/observational, no randomization of when stimulation was state-matched versus not. Relevance: this is the best available methodological template for RCS08 — it demonstrates state-dependence using exactly the kind of chronic, continuously logged device data (detections, stimulation timestamps, long-term background trend) that the Percept RC and its stored recordings already provide, and it used a statistical framework (state defined by cyclical trend position, effect defined by a subsequent-activity change) that is directly portable.

**Chiang S, Khambhati AN, Tcheng TK, et al. "State-dependent effects of responsive neurostimulation depend on seizure localization." *Brain*. 2025;148(2):521–532. doi:10.1093/brain/awae240. PMID: 39052924.**
Population: a larger RNS cohort than the 2021 paper, stratified by seizure-onset localization (mesiotemporal versus neocortical). Method: extended the 2021 state-dependence framework, testing whether the *type* of state-dependence (which stimulation parameter, bipolar versus monopolar) differed by where in the brain the seizures originated. Finding: state-dependent effectiveness was present in both localizations but took different forms — bipolar stimulation showed more state-dependent variance for neocortical epilepsies, monopolar for mesiotemporal — meaning the state-dependence phenomenon itself is not uniform even within one disease and one device; it interacts with anatomy/circuit type. Limitation: epilepsy, retrospective. Relevance: a caution against assuming RCS08's left-lead 21.5–27.5 Hz state-dependence (if it exists) would look like the right-lead's, or like Parkinson's beta, or like OCD ventral-striatal periodicity — the *form* state-dependence takes may be specific to the circuit and stimulation configuration, meaning RCS08's own left- and right-lead data may need to be tested and characterized separately rather than pooled (which is already this project's practice per decision 157/199).

## What could masquerade as state dependence

The PI's hypothesis makes a specific, falsifiable claim: that the *brain's own ongoing activity when a current is set* changes what that current subsequently does to pain. Several other processes produce the same top-level symptom — "a fixed setting's apparent benefit changed across blocks of time" — without requiring any true state-by-current interaction. Each must be considered and, where possible, measured and subtracted before crediting state dependence.

1. **Regression to the mean.** If a setting was chosen or a block boundary was placed *because* pain was unusually good or bad at that time (which is exactly how clinical titration works — settings are adjusted when things look good or bad), later re-measurement will drift back toward the patient's average regardless of any true causal change (Barnett AG, van der Pols JC, Dobson AJ. "Regression to the mean: what it is and how to deal with it." *Int J Epidemiol*. 2005;34(1):215–220. doi:10.1093/ije/dyh299. PMID: 15333621). This is a strong candidate explanation for the specific pattern already measured on RCS08 (decision 253): the left-leg 55 Hz/60-160µs surface's held-out coverage failure and its most-delivered setting's objective falling from 1.51 to 0.53 to -0.06 across blocks could be regression to the mean if that setting was more likely to be revisited, or held longest, right after a period when it looked unusually good.
2. **A decaying placebo/expectation response.** Neurostimulation trials for pain show large placebo arms (one review found roughly 34–50% pain reduction across active low-frequency, active 1 kHz, and sham arms), and expectation is a documented, measurable modulator of these responses over time, not a one-time event (search results on placebo/expectation in stimulation trials, sham spinal cord stimulation literature). A patient's initial enthusiasm for an unchanged new setting can fade over weeks in a way that looks exactly like "the current stopped working," entirely independent of any change in the underlying neural response to the electrical field.
3. **Weekday/weekend and time-of-day cycles, already measured in this project's own data.** Decision 246's time-of-day diagnostic found that pain runs higher at weekends on RCS08 across multiple pain scales (NRS +0.30/10, VAS +3.9/100, McGill +1.9, back VAS +3.4, all with p≤0.022), and two sensing pairs (L 0-2+, R 1-3+ — not the clinically usable pairs) show strong daily band-power cycles peaking near midnight. If the days sampled in one "block of time" happen to be weekend-heavy and the next block weekday-heavy, an apparent shift in the current-pain relationship can be entirely a calendar artifact rather than a brain-state one. This project's own rule that "time is modeled nowhere" (decisions 193–196) was a deliberate choice under an assumption (drift = stimulation effect in a patient years into disease) that this same evidence base (weekend effect, "Painful Mondays") now puts some pressure on for at least the *pain-reporting* side, independent of any neural mechanism.
4. **Carry-over from the previous setting.** In crossover and N-of-1 trial methodology, a treatment period's apparent effect is confounded with lingering effects of whatever came before it unless a washout period is used; "inserting a washout period... is the most common method to reduce or even eliminate the effect of carry-over" (search results on N-of-1 trial design). RCS08's settings changes are not randomized and typically have no formal washout, so a block that immediately follows a very different current may show apparent instability that is really incomplete equilibration, not moment-to-moment state dependence.
5. **The patient's own rating habits and non-independence of repeated measures.** Repeated ratings from one person in a short window are correlated with each other (a bad morning produces several bad ratings in a row) rather than independent draws; naive models that treat each rating as an independent sample of "the effect of the current right now" will overstate both the apparent signal and its apparent instability across blocks. This is a general statistical-conclusion-validity concern (see Critical Analysis, below), not evidence against the PI's hypothesis, but it means any state-dependence test must build in a per-episode or per-day random effect before crediting a block-to-block difference to "state."
6. **Day-to-day pain-intensity variability being large and clinically meaningful on its own.** Pagé MG, Gauvin L, Sylvestre MP, Nitulescu R, Dyachenko A, Choinière M. "An Ecological Momentary Assessment Study of Pain Intensity Variability: Ascertaining Extent, Predictors, and Associations With Quality of Life, Interference and Health Care Utilization Among Individuals Living With Chronic Low Back Pain." *J Pain*. 2022;23(7):1151–1166. doi:10.1016/j.jpain.2022.01.001. PMID: 35074499. This paper documents that intraindividual pain-intensity variability is large and clinically important in its own right in a chronic low back pain population resembling RCS08's presentation, and separately notes that ignoring such variability can produce false-positive directionality and magnitude errors in exactly the kind of block-comparison analysis this project has been running. Limitation: chronic low back pain cohort, not DBS-implanted, no neural recording — cited here purely for the general-population base rate of pain variability, not as DBS evidence. Relevance: some fraction of the block-to-block "objective" swing on RCS08 may simply be the ordinary variance chronic pain has on its own, at a scale this study helps calibrate, before any DBS-specific mechanism needs to be invoked.

## Testable predictions in RCS08's data — concrete analyses

Each analysis below states what data it needs, what a supporting result would look like, and what a null/refuting result would look like. All are DEFER/HEDGED in the sense that none has been run on the live record; these are proposals, not findings.

### T1. Pre-onset band power as a moderator of the ramp's current-to-pain relationship, on the titration ladder

- **What it tests:** whether the level (and/or variance) of the pain-associated left-lead bands (21.5–27.5 Hz, decisions 229/233/236) in the few minutes *before* a current step predicts whether that step's pain response looks stronger, weaker, or reversed.
- **Data needed:** the settled-step current-and-pain-report table the ladder analysis already builds (`titration_readings.py`, decision 237, and the run-level table from decision 213), plus each step's own pre-step band power from the immediately preceding recording window (available from the device's continuous band-power stream already ingested).
- **Design:** for each settled step in the ladder (order of 15–30 per session, per decisions 230/236), regress the step's pain reading on current, pre-step band power, and their interaction, with a random effect (or fixed block term) for session/day; a state-dependence-supporting result is a significant interaction term whose sign/direction is stable across at least two independent ladder sessions. Given the small number of steps per session, this is likely underpowered within a single ladder (order of 15–30 observations, one interaction term plus several controls) — the honest expectation, stated up front, is a wide confidence interval, not a crisp answer, from one session alone; power grows only by pooling multiple ladder sessions run at different times, which requires more titration visits (already flagged as needed in decisions 239/251/255).
- **What would support the hypothesis:** an interaction term whose 95% interval excludes zero and whose direction is consistent across at least two sessions — e.g., steps that begin from a high-power pre-step state show a *flatter or reversed* current-pain slope compared to steps beginning from a low-power pre-step state.
- **What would refute or fail to support it:** an interaction interval that includes zero with reasonable precision (not just "not significant because of six data points"), or a direction that flips between sessions in a way better explained by which leg of the ladder (rising vs. falling, per decision 213) it was.
- **What could masquerade as a positive result here:** the current itself often drives the band power (the entire premise of E1, decisions 126/213), so "pre-step band power" already partly encodes "what the current recently was," not an independent state; this needs the current-adjusted band power (decision 234's `AdjustForStimCurrent`, or the shaped-covariate removal of decision 241) as the moderator, not the raw one, or the analysis simply re-discovers autocorrelation in current.

### T2. Held-out block-of-time miss, partitioned by whether the missed block's most-delivered setting was preceded by an unusually good or unusually bad stretch

- **What it tests:** whether decision 253's finding (the left-leg 55 Hz surface "moves between blocks of time," with the most-delivered setting's objective falling 1.51→0.53→−0.06, p=0.0006) is regression to the mean (setting revisited after a good stretch, therefore looking worse next time) rather than a change in the brain's responsiveness to that setting.
- **Data needed:** the same purged, time-blocked fold data decision 240/253 already computed, plus — newly — the pain trajectory in the days immediately preceding each time a given setting was reselected or re-delivered.
- **Design:** for the L1.6/R1.2 mA, 55 Hz setting (the specific one already flagged), check whether each of the three blocks in decision 253 was preceded by a period of unusually good pain (which would predict a regression-to-the-mean fall on re-test) or by a period of unusually bad pain (which would predict a regression-to-the-mean rise, the opposite of what was found) — if the pattern matches "preceded by good, followed by worse," regression to the mean is a live, parsimonious alternative and should be reported alongside any state-dependence claim, not instead of it (both can be true).
- **Supporting result for true state dependence, not regression:** the fall in the setting's objective is *not* preceded by an unusually good stretch (ruling out the simplest regression story), and/or a plausible band-power state differs measurably between the three blocks in a way that does not simply track the pain trend itself (i.e., is not circular).
- **Refuting result:** the fall tracks a simple "reselected because it looked good, therefore fell back" pattern with no independent neural signature.

### T3. Weekend/weekday and time-of-day stratification of the same E1/pooled-slope analyses already run

- **What it tests:** whether the apparent block-to-block instability (decision 253) or the harder-to-explain positive left-lead band correlations (decisions 229/234) are partly weekend-composition artifacts.
- **Data needed:** nothing new — the therapy-settings and pain-report tables already carry timestamps; this simply requires re-running the existing block/fold definitions stratified by, or explicitly controlling for, weekend-vs-weekday and the reporting hour, exactly as decision 246's time-of-day diagnostic did for the correlation analysis (which found the weekend effect moved the L 1-3+ correlation by at most 0.019 — "real in direction, negligible in size").
- **Supporting result for a genuine state effect surviving this control:** the block-to-block objective swing (1.51→0.53→−0.06) and/or the interaction terms in T1 persist essentially unchanged after adding a weekend/weekday and hour-of-day covariate.
- **Refuting result:** the swing shrinks substantially, or the sign pattern changes, once weekend composition per block is accounted for — in which case at least part of "the same current didn't do the same thing" is "the same current was tested on a different mix of good and bad days," a calendar artifact rather than a neural one.

### T4. A switching/regime model on the continuous band-power stream, tested for whether regime occupancy (not current) predicts the pain relationship

- **What it tests:** a stronger, more mechanistic version of the hypothesis — that the pain-associated bands occupy a small number of discrete "regimes" (as in Kirkby et al. 2018's network-state framing, or a hidden Markov model as reviewed in the depression-EEG HMM literature), and that the band-to-pain relationship, or the current-to-band relationship, differs by which regime is active.
- **Data needed:** the continuous, unlabeled band-power stream already stored (the raw kind `biomarker_psd_matrix`), long enough stretches of it to fit a multi-state model (order of thousands of windows, which RCS08 has), and the existing pain-report and current-settings tables to test the fitted regimes against afterward (regimes should be fit *blind* to pain and current, then tested against them, to avoid circularity).
- **Design:** fit a small number of states (2–4, chosen by held-out likelihood, not by eye) to the band-power time series using a hidden Markov or Gaussian-mixture switching model with time-blocked cross-validation (reusing this project's own purged-block-fold framework, decision 240); then, held out, test whether regime membership predicts pain-report residuals beyond current and whether the current-to-band-power slope (E1) differs by regime.
- **What would support the hypothesis:** a fitted regime whose occupancy predicts pain beyond current, and/or a current-to-band slope that is credibly different (non-overlapping intervals) between regimes.
- **What would refute it:** the best-fitting number of regimes on held-out data is one (i.e., no evidence for discrete states at all), or regimes exist but do not predict anything beyond what current and time-of-day already explain.
- **Sample size caveat, stated plainly:** switching-state models are data-hungry, and this project's own confound-diagnostic work (decision 240) already found that even a simpler, single-state model of pain from band power failed to beat its own null out of sample (best AUC 0.701 against a null 95th of 0.713, on the richest available pair) — a multi-state model, with more free parameters, is *more* likely to overfit on this size of record unless the number of states is kept very small and validated exactly as rigorously.

### T5. The three-block-of-holds design already proposed for the exploratory ladder (decision 230/236), read as a direct within-session state test

- **What it tests:** the titration card's own "part B" (three 5-minute holds off/on/off at the top tolerated current, blind to the patient, with a rating every minute, decision 230) is, without modification, close to an ideal design for testing short-timescale state dependence, because it holds the current fixed and repeats it, which none of the open-loop clinical record does.
- **Data needed:** the band power and ratings collected during that hold protocol, once run (its live-watch step was still pending as of decision 248).
- **Design:** compare the band power in the minute immediately before the "on" period starts, across repeated instances of the same on/off transition (this design gives at most one or two repeats per visit, so it accrues power only across many visits) and test whether pre-transition band level predicts the size of the pain change during the hold.
- **What would support the hypothesis:** a hold that starts from a "high" pre-transition state shows a different pain trajectory than one starting from a "low" state.
- **Honest limitation:** with only one or two current-onset transitions per visit, this cannot resolve the question in one session; it is a data-collection recommendation for the next several visits (consistent with decision 239's finding that the record generally needs more repeated settings, not just more distinct ones), not an analysis that can be run today.

## Implications for a single-band, single-threshold controller

The Parkinson's adaptive-DBS literature (Little et al. 2013; Tinkhauser et al. 2017) is the direct precedent for the architecture a closed-loop pain controller on RCS08 would use: read one band's power, compare it to one fixed threshold, stimulate (or change stimulation) accordingly. Three things from the evidence above bear on that design:

1. **A single fixed threshold assumes the band-to-symptom mapping is stationary; several of the strongest analogues found here say it is not.** Little et al. (2013) succeeded with a single beta threshold in an *acute*, single-session setting; Tinkhauser et al. (2017) then showed that even beta's clinically relevant information is carried by burst duration, a temporal-pattern feature, not simply instantaneous level — meaning even the best-established single-band controller in the field has already moved conceptually past "one level, one threshold" toward pattern-aware triggering. Provenza et al. (2024) found that in a comparable chronic DBS/sensing population (OCD, ventral striatum), it was the *change in the biomarker's temporal structure over weeks*, not a fixed instantaneous crossing, that tracked treatment response. If RCS08's 21.5–27.5 Hz bands behave similarly, a controller keyed to one static threshold on those bands would be blind to exactly the dimension (temporal/regime structure) that carries the clinically relevant information, and could just as easily fire during a "good" instance of high power as a "bad" one.
2. **This project's own confound analysis already shows the danger concretely.** Decision 234 and the broader panel review (decision 232) found that on the left lead, the bands proposed as pain-positive lose their positive interval once the concurrent stimulation current is statistically removed, and decision 240 found that no set of left- or right-lead bands beats its own out-of-sample null at predicting pain at all (best AUC 0.701 vs. a null 95th of 0.713). A single-band-single-threshold controller built on a band whose apparent pain relationship is actually borrowed from the current already in force would, when it changes the current, do the equivalent of Cagnan/Holt's "opposite phase" pulse: it could push the system in the wrong direction, not merely fail to help, because the trigger it reads is partly an echo of its own actuator (current) rather than an independent read of pain state — a live version of the store's own provenance-cycle refusal rule (`ARCHITECTURE_cache_store.md` §3.3: "a module cannot consume a product its own output helped produce"), here applying to the physiology rather than the code.
3. **State-dependence work in TMS (Schaworonkow et al. 2019) found the effect is strongest near threshold, not at extremes** — an argument for expecting any true pain-relevant state-dependence on RCS08, if it exists, to show up most clearly *near whatever current level is closest to a genuine dose-response inflection* (the "turn-over" current explored in decision 241's shape analysis, itself only suggestively supported, 95% interval −0.079 to +0.486 on the squared term), and to be weak or invisible at either very low or ceiling currents — meaning a search for state-dependence across the *whole* tested current range is less likely to find it than a search focused near that inflection, if one is eventually established with more data.
4. **A pragmatic middle path already exists in the literature and in this project's own toolkit**: rather than requiring a full mechanistic state model before building anything, both the RNS-epilepsy work (Chiang et al. 2021, 2025) and this project's own confound-gate approach (decision 240's "reported plainly AND with the current taken out, never blocking") show that a controller or its evaluation can carry a state-awareness *flag* — report whether the current reading looks like it is coming from a state resembling past "band moves for reasons unrelated to current" episodes — without needing to solve the full switching-model problem first, and, per this project's stated practice, doing so as a warning rather than a gate until the evidence is much stronger.
