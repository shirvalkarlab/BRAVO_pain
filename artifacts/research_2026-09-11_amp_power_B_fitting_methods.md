# Statistical Methods for Fitting Non-Monotonic Amplitude–Response Curves and Estimating the Peak Location

> Research report
> Researcher: worker-research | Date: 2026-09-11
> Confidence: MIXED — core statistical methods (quadratic/Fieller, drc hormesis models, segmented regression, Bayesian optimization for DBS) are backed by primary methodological papers and software documentation (HIGH); DBS-LFP-specific applications of these exact methods are sparse in the literature, so several links between "the general method" and "its use on amplitude-vs-LFP-power data" are inferred rather than directly documented (LOW–MEDIUM).

## Key Findings

1. **No single "amplitude-power peak" statistical framework is standard in DBS-LFP work.** The DBS literature mostly reports beta-power *suppression* as amplitude increases (a monotonic or saturating relationship), not an inverted-U. Non-monotonic ("inverted-U" or "hormetic") amplitude–response curve-fitting is a well-developed methodology in pharmacology/toxicology (dose-response) and response-surface engineering, and these are the fields DBS researchers would need to borrow from. (MEDIUM — based on absence of DBS-specific hits despite multiple targeted searches; another worker is covering DBS-LFP empirical findings directly and may have found examples this search missed.)

2. **Quadratic fits with Fieller's theorem are the classical, closed-form way to get a peak location plus a confidence interval**, and Fieller intervals are recommended over the simpler delta-method interval because they are exact under normality and have documented better coverage, especially when the curvature term is not precisely estimated (HIGH; Fieller 1954, J. Royal Statist. Soc. B).

3. **The pharmacology field has purpose-built parametric models for rise-then-fall ("hormetic"/biphasic) dose-response curves** — the Brain-Cousens (1989) and Cedergreen-Ritz-Streibig (2005) models — implemented in the R package `drc`, which also computes the peak (maximal-response) dose and its standard error via the delta method (HIGH).

4. **Piecewise-linear/segmented regression (Muggeo's `segmented` R package) is the standard tool when the relationship is better described as two (or more) straight-line pieces meeting at a "breakpoint" than as a smooth curve**, and it now supports both a fast approximate CI and a more accurate score-based CI for the breakpoint location (Muggeo 2003, 2017) (HIGH).

5. **Bayesian optimization (Gaussian-process-based) is the dominant method actually used in the DBS literature for finding an optimal stimulation parameter with few samples** — used by Grado et al. (2018), Sarikhani et al. (2019, 2022), and Cole et al./SAFE-OPT (2024) — but these systems are built to *find* an optimum efficiently via active sampling, not to statistically characterize a pre-collected amplitude-sweep dataset; they are a different use case from curve-fitting a fixed dataset (HIGH for what these papers do; the distinction is my analytical note, LOW-confidence as a claim about applicability to your dataset).

6. **For mixed-effects/repeated-visit data (several sessions each contributing points around their own baseline), the standard approach is a per-subject or per-visit random intercept added to whatever curve model is chosen** (quadratic, segmented, or spline), with the population-level peak read off the fixed-effects curve; Muggeo's `segmented` package explicitly supports random-effect breakpoints (Muggeo 2014), and mixed-effects Gaussian process regression is documented for dose-response curves (Shi et al.) (MEDIUM-HIGH).

7. **Distinguishing "a real bend" from noise requires either a formal curvature test (e.g., response-surface-methodology's center-point curvature test comparing factorial-point means to center-point means) or a model-comparison approach (AIC/BIC/likelihood-ratio comparing linear vs. quadratic/segmented fits)**; classical response-surface-methodology guidance calls for replicated center points plus points spread across at least 3 distinct levels (5 is typical for a proper quadratic/central-composite design) (MEDIUM-HIGH — general DOE guidance, not DBS-specific).

8. **Detecting two peaks (M-shaped curves) is the least standardized area.** Purpose-built tools exist — the "Dr Fit" automated multiphasic dose-response fitting/model-selection software (Di Veroli et al. 2015), mixture-of-quadratics/segmented models with 2+ breakpoints, restricted cubic splines with data-driven knot selection, and SiZer (significant-zero-crossings-of-derivatives) scale-space maps for identifying the number of significant local features in a smoothed curve — but there is no single agreed default, and Hartigan's dip test (commonly cited for "bimodality") tests unimodality of a *probability density/histogram*, not the shape of a fitted regression curve, so it is only indirectly applicable here (HIGH on what each tool does individually; MEDIUM on how well they are documented as an integrated workflow).

## When to Prefer Which Method (Plain-English Decision Guidance)

- **You have few amplitude levels (≤5–6), a clean single hump is expected, and you want a simple, fast answer with a defensible confidence interval**: fit a quadratic and use Fieller's theorem for the peak's confidence interval. Fieller is preferred over the simpler "delta method" because it is more honest about uncertainty when your estimate of the curvature (how sharply the response bends) is itself uncertain — which is the normal situation with a handful of amplitude levels.

- **You suspect the response rises then plateaus/falls off in a genuinely non-symmetric, sigmoid-like way (not a simple parabola) — e.g., there's a clear "floor" and "ceiling" to the response**: use a hormesis dose-response model (Brain-Cousens or Cedergreen-Ritz-Streibig) via the `drc` R package. These are built specifically for "rise then standard sigmoid decline" shapes and give you an effective-dose/peak estimate with a standard error, the same way pharmacologists estimate an EC50.

- **You think the relationship looks like two connected straight lines (a "knee" or "elbow") rather than a smooth curve — e.g., LFP power rises steadily then abruptly plateaus or reverses at some amplitude**: use segmented/piecewise-linear regression (`segmented` R package). It is designed exactly for "where does the slope change" questions and gives you a breakpoint location plus a confidence interval, along with a formal test for whether a breakpoint exists at all (Davies' test) versus just noise.

- **You don't want to assume any particular shape at all, and you have a reasonable number of points (roughly 10+) spread across the range**: use a nonparametric method — Gaussian process regression if you also want a principled uncertainty band and can specify a smoothness assumption (kernel), or local regression (loess) if you just want a flexible visual fit. If you're confident the curve truly has just one hump (rises then falls, no wiggles), isotonic/unimodal regression is a good middle ground — it's less flexible (hence needs less data) than a fully free-form GP or loess fit, because it bakes in the single-peak assumption as a constraint rather than estimating it from data.

- **You are actively collecting data and want to find the best amplitude in as few stimulation trials as possible (rather than characterizing a fixed, already-collected sweep)**: Bayesian optimization (GP-based active sampling), as used in Grado et al. (2018), Sarikhani et al. (2019, 2022), and Cole et al./SAFE-OPT (2024). This is a fundamentally different use case from fitting a curve to a fixed dataset — it's a sequential search strategy. If safety/side-effect limits matter (e.g., don't want to test amplitudes that cause unwanted side effects), the SAFE-OPT constrained variant is the more directly relevant reference.

- **You have data from multiple visits/sessions, each with its own baseline LFP power level**: add a random intercept (and possibly random slope/breakpoint) for visit/session to whichever curve model you choose. Don't just pool all points into a single curve fit ignoring session — that risks conflating between-session baseline differences with the amplitude effect itself, and will bias your peak-location estimate if baseline and amplitude range happen to be correlated across sessions.

- **You are unsure whether an apparent bend is real or is just noise, and want a formal test before trusting a peak estimate at all**: use a curvature test — either the classical response-surface-methodology center-point test (compare the mean response at replicated middle-amplitude points to what a straight line through the outer points would predict) or a model-comparison approach (fit both a straight line and a quadratic/segmented model, compare via AIC/BIC or a likelihood-ratio test, and only trust the curved model if it's a significantly better fit).

- **You suspect there might be two separate peaks (an M-shape), not one**: this is the least standardized situation. Start by fitting a flexible model (spline via `mgcv::gam`, or a 2-breakpoint segmented model) and use model selection (AIC/BIC) to compare against the single-peak version. A SiZer map can help visually confirm whether the "second peak" is a robust feature across a range of smoothing bandwidths or an artifact of one particular smoothing choice. Purpose-built multiphasic dose-response software (Dr Fit, Di Veroli et al. 2015) automates this model-selection process for pharmacology-style data and may be adaptable. This situation intrinsically needs substantially more data points (roughly double what's needed for a single peak) because you must resolve two rises, two falls, and the dip between them.

## Handling Several Visits with Different Baselines

The core statistical tool for "several sessions/visits, each contributing amplitude-response points but each with its own baseline LFP level," is a **mixed-effects (hierarchical/multilevel) model**: the shape of the amplitude-response curve (quadratic, segmented, or spline) is fit as the population-level "fixed effect," and each visit gets its own random intercept (and, if there's evidence baselines differ enough, potentially its own random slope or even a random peak location).

- For a **quadratic** fit: y_ij = (a + u_j) + b·x_ij + c·x_ij² + ε_ij, where u_j is a random intercept for visit j drawn from a normal distribution with its own variance. The population peak is still −b/(2c), computed from the fixed-effect coefficients only, with its Fieller/delta/bootstrap CI computed from the fixed-effects covariance matrix (ignoring the random-intercept variance, since intercept shifts don't move the peak's x-location for a purely additive random intercept — this is a mathematically convenient property of additive-intercept quadratics: a vertical shift does not move the vertex). This is documented generally in the mixed-model literature (e.g., Laird-Ware random-intercept framework) though not in a source specifically about amplitude-vs-LFP-power sweeps (MEDIUM confidence — this is a mathematical inference from the model structure, not something I found stated explicitly in a DBS-specific paper).

- For **segmented regression**: Muggeo explicitly extended the `segmented` package to support **random-effects breakpoints** — i.e., each subject/visit can have not just its own intercept but potentially its own breakpoint location, shrunk toward a population breakpoint (Muggeo, V.M.R., Muggeo, "Segmented mixed models with random changepoints: a maximum likelihood approach with a modified EM algorithm," *Statistical Modelling*, 2014). This is directly relevant when you suspect the "bend point" amplitude itself might vary somewhat across sessions.

- For **Gaussian process regression**: a "mixed-effects GP" formulation has been proposed (Shi, J.Q. et al., "Mixed-effects Gaussian process functional regression models with application to dose-response curve prediction," *Statistics in Medicine*) where a population-mean GP curve is combined with subject-specific deviation curves — conceptually the nonparametric analogue of a random-intercept/random-slope mixed model.

- **Practical alternative when a full mixed-effects fit is impractical**: center or baseline-correct each visit's data (subtract that visit's own baseline/zero-amplitude response, or its own mean) before pooling all points into a single curve fit. This is a simpler but less statistically rigorous approach than a proper mixed-effects model, since it treats the baseline correction as fixed and known rather than estimated with its own uncertainty, which will understate the true uncertainty in the pooled peak estimate.

## Detecting Two Peaks (M-Shaped Curves)

This is the area with the least methodological consensus found in this search. Three complementary approaches:

1. **Model-comparison approach**: Fit a single-peak model (one quadratic, or a 2-segment/1-breakpoint model) and a two-peak model (two quadratics joined, or a multi-segment/2-breakpoint model, or a spline with enough knots to represent two humps), then compare via AIC/BIC or a likelihood-ratio test. Caveat: a naive LRT comparing models with a different number of breakpoints does not follow a standard chi-squared distribution because the breakpoint location parameter is not identified under the null (this is the same "changepoint testing" identifiability problem addressed by Davies' test in the `segmented` package framework).

2. **Purpose-built multiphasic dose-response software**: Di Veroli et al. (2015), *Scientific Reports* 5:14701 (DOI: 10.1038/srep14701), built "Dr Fit," free software that automatically generates and ranks (via a model-selection criterion) dose-response models with 1, 2, or more inflection points/phases, and applied it to over 11,000 dose-response curves, finding ~28% were better described by a multiphasic than a standard single-Hill model. This is the closest thing to a purpose-built, validated tool for exactly this problem, though it comes from cancer pharmacology, not neurostimulation.

3. **SiZer scale-space maps** (Chaudhuri & Marron, 1999): rather than committing to a single smoothing bandwidth and asking "does this fit show two peaks," SiZer produces a map across a whole range of bandwidths showing where the curve's slope is significantly positive, negative, or ambiguous. A genuine second peak should show up as a consistent "rise-then-fall" (blue-then-red) signature across a reasonable range of bandwidths, not just at one arbitrary smoothing choice — this guards against over-interpreting noise as a second peak.

**Not directly applicable, despite being commonly cited for "bimodality":** Hartigan's dip test (Hartigan & Hartigan, 1985, *Annals of Statistics*) tests whether a **sample of data values** (e.g., a histogram or density) is unimodal — it is built for testing whether a *distribution of observations* has one mode or several, not for testing whether a *fitted regression curve relating amplitude to response* has one or two peaks. Applying it directly to amplitude-response data would require reframing the question (e.g., testing the distribution of bootstrap-resampled peak-location estimates for bimodality, which is a stretch), so it should not be used as a first-choice tool for this specific curve-shape question.

## Contradictions

- **Delta method vs. Fieller method for quadratic-vertex CIs**: Sources agree the Fieller interval is generally more reliable/wider, but they disagree on how large the difference is in practice — some sources frame it as a minor refinement, while the more technical source ("Confidence bounds for the extremum determined by a quadratic regression," ResearchGate-indexed) frames it as "dramatically better coverage" when the quadratic coefficient is not estimated with great precision. I was not able to fully verify which characterization is more accurate without reading the full paper behind a paywall (MEDIUM confidence on the general direction of the claim; LOW confidence on the exact magnitude of the difference).
