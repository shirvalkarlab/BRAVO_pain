# Alternative biomarker-detection and symptom-tracking methods for RCS08

> Research report
> Researcher: worker-research | Date: 2026-09-22
> Confidence: MIXED — the codebase claims are high confidence (read from the files named); the literature claims are medium confidence (secondary summaries of primary papers, not full-text verification of every number); several proposals depend on RCS08 data properties (rating count, ladder count) that would need a live run to confirm before building

## One-page summary

The Percept RC can only run one thing in closed loop: **one band's power, averaged over up to 30 seconds, compared with one or two switching values, driving amplitude up or down** (`DEVICE_percept_rc.md` §2; `DecodeCommon/device_ranges.py`). It cannot run a multi-feature classifier, cannot threshold a peak's shape, cannot combine two contacts, and cannot see the clock. Every method below is judged against that one fact. This project's current method — a Pearson-correlation heat map of 22 frequency bands against pain ratings, with a block-bootstrap interval and a shuffle-derived p-value corrected across bands (`BRAVO/modules/Biomarkers/routines/analytics.py`, `stats_utils.py`) — already respects the constraint by construction: it screens single bands one at a time. The question this report answers is what else could improve on that screen, either by picking a *better* single band (the deployable output stays the same shape) or by making better use of the sparse pain ratings that feed it.

Four ideas are worth building. First, a **multivariate offline decoder** (in the spirit of Shirvalkar 2023 and the Percept-specific work reviewed here) run only as a *selection* tool: fit a richer model across many bands and both leads, then hand the device only the single band that model leans on most — never deploy the model itself. Second, a **latent-state model of the pain ratings themselves** (a two-state or continuous hidden Markov model, or a simple Bayesian smoother) built to de-noise the sparse, bursty REDCap ratings before they are correlated against band power — this treats the sparse-and-noisy label as the problem, which the current grid does not. Third, an **aperiodic/periodic spectral split (FOOOF/specparam)** used only to explain *why* a band correlates, never to threshold a shape the device cannot compute — this is explicitly why decision 206 deleted the aperiodic fit from this codebase, and the same objection applies to any spectral-shape feature. Fourth, a **change-point or event-aligned re-analysis of the titration ladders**, which is a within-visit, sparse-rating-tolerant way to ask whether a band moves *because of* stimulation rather than merely alongside pain — the raw ingredient for this already exists in `ClosedLoopDeployment/design_rule.py`'s Kalman/local-level fit of band power, though that machinery today models the *signal's own noise* to place a threshold, not pain.

None of the four requires touching the device interface, which does not exist in this platform (`DEVICE_percept_rc.md` §1). All four can plug into the existing store and grid without reversing a standing decision, provided the log-power ban (202), the raw-power device-threshold rule, the one-band rule (199, 210, 217) and "time is modelled nowhere" (193-196) are respected — two of the proposals below explicitly bend on time (a change-point model finds a moment, and a Kalman smoother has a memory), and each says why that is not a reversal of decision 196: they estimate a *level*, not a *drift-with-age* term, and they operate on a demonstration/screening panel, not the deployed switching value.

## 2. Top five, described in plain language

### 2.1 Multivariate offline decoder used only to pick which single band to threshold

**The idea.** Instead of screening 22 bands one at a time with a straight-line correlation, fit one model that looks at every band on both sides of the brain at once and asks which one band, alone, carries almost all of the information the full model has. This is the approach used in the first paper to predict a chronic pain state from implanted brain recordings in humans (Shirvalkar et al., 2023, *Nature Neuroscience*, four participants, months of ambulatory recording paired with self-reports) [1], and in the mood-decoding work from the Shanechi lab that built state-space models across several implanted brain sites and predicted self-reported mood between infrequent reports (Sani et al., 2018, *Nature Biotechnology*) [2]. Neither device in those studies could act on the model directly — the point here is the same: the multivariate model never leaves the laptop. Its only output is a ranking of single bands by how much a simple, single-number readout of them would have cost the full model in accuracy.

**The model.** A regularized logistic or random-forest classifier (median split of the chosen pain metric, the same split the grid already uses) trained on all 22 band centres times both sensing contacts times however many signal lengths are stored, with cross-validation folds built along time (never a random shuffle, because adjacent 3-second tiles are not independent — the same reasoning behind this project's block bootstrap, `stats_utils.py`). After fitting, compute each feature's marginal contribution (a permutation-importance or a drop-one-feature test) and compare the *single best feature's own AUC* against the *full model's AUC*. If they are close, a single band really does carry most of what the richer model has, which supports staying on this device. If they are far apart, that is itself a finding worth reporting: the richest available model still cannot be reduced to one number without a real loss, and the clinician should know that before committing to a band.

**Using RCS08's data.** RCS08 has bilateral leads, several sensing contacts, 22 frequency bands each, nine window lengths, and a REDCap left-leg VAS with clinic-sheet ratings layered in (decision 186). The tile cache (`BRAVO/modules/CacheStore`) already holds the band-power tiles this would read; nothing new has to be recorded. Decision 229's exploratory search already ran something adjacent — 252 settings of matching-window and direction knobs on the left 1-3+ contact — and found no band clears family-wise significance without clinic sheets, and with clinic sheets on, 11-22 of 22 bands move together, which reads as a global effect rather than a specific band (see decision 229 and its "read as a lead, not a band to program" caveat). A multivariate decoder run on the same data would either sharpen that same warning (many correlated bands, no one of them special) or surface a genuinely different band the univariate grid underweights because its individual r is modest but its interaction with another band is not (the interaction itself would still not be deployable — only the single best marginal feature would be).

**The statistic and how a clinician reads it.** Two numbers: the full model's cross-validated AUC (chance is 0.5, never 0), and the best single feature's own AUC. A small gap ("the richest model reaches 0.71, and the single best band alone reaches 0.68") says the one-band device is not leaving much on the table. A large gap ("the richest model reaches 0.71, the best single band reaches 0.54") says the device's one-band limit is the binding constraint on this participant, which is a fact worth stating plainly rather than papering over with a hopeful correlation.

**Failure modes.** With sparse ratings (RCS08's daily and clinic-visit counts), a rich multivariate model overfits easily; the AUC gap itself needs the same block-bootstrap or permutation confidence interval the grid already applies, not a point estimate. A model that leans on two contacts on the same side to reach its accuracy is reporting an effect the device cannot use at all (it senses one pair), so the "single best feature" must be restricted to features that are themselves single, deployable columns — otherwise the comparison is unfair to begin with.

**Exists in code today?** No. `Biomarkers/routines/analytics.py` builds only univariate, per-band statistics; nothing in the repository fits a joint model across bands for selection purposes. Reached by no page.

### 2.2 Two-state (or few-state) hidden Markov model of the pain ratings, used to denoise the label before correlating

**The idea.** REDCap ratings and clinic-sheet scores are sparse, bursty, and noisy: a single 0-10 number reported once or a few times a day, subject to the peak/end recall bias documented in the ecological-momentary-assessment literature (Stone, Schwartz, Broderick and Shiffman, 2005, showing that the *variability* of momentary pain predicts how a person recalls their week's pain, a salience-heuristic effect measurable within one day [9]; Schneider, Stone, Schwartz and Broderick's related work on peak/end effects in daily pain and fatigue recall [10]). Rather than treat every rating as an unbiased point value of "true pain right now" — which is what `pain_series` in `availability.py` does — model the rating series itself as noisy observations of a smaller number of hidden states (for instance "flare" and "baseline"), each with its own typical duration (its dwell time) and its own chance of a misreported score. This is a long-established approach for irregular self-report or clinical-state data with measurement error and no gold-standard label (Bureau et al., 2003, *Statistics in Medicine*, continuous-time hidden Markov models for misclassified disease outcomes [7]; Benoit et al., 2016, a hidden Markov model for longitudinal outcomes with possible misclassification [8]).

**The model.** A hidden Markov model with two or three states over calendar time, fit on the rating series alone (no neural data enters the fit). Each state has a mean pain level and its own reporting-noise variance; the Markov chain has a transition matrix that encodes how long a "flare" typically lasts before the patient returns to "baseline." Fitting produces, for every recorded rating, a smoothed probability of being in each state — a soft, denoised label — instead of the raw number.

**Using RCS08's data.** RCS08's rating history spans REDCap daily entries plus clinic-visit sheets (decision 182, synced daily). The HMM needs only that series; it is a light addition sitting entirely on the label side, independent of everything the Biomarkers grid does with signal. Once fit, the grid could be re-run twice — once against the raw rating (today's baseline) and once against the smoothed state probability — and the two Pearson-r values compared band by band. A band whose r rises meaningfully under the smoothed label is a band where the raw rating's noise, not a weak true relationship, was suppressing the correlation; a band whose r does not move is unaffected either way.

**The statistic and how a clinician reads it.** Two heat maps side by side, same 22 bands, same colour scale: "correlation against the reported score" and "correlation against the smoothed pain state." The clinician reads whichever bands change most between the two and treats a rise as: "removing likely reporting noise from the pain score made this band look more consistent," never as: "this band is now established" — the smoothed label still needs its own bootstrap interval and the same Benjamini-Hochberg correction the raw-rating grid already applies (`stats_utils.py: bh_fdr`).

**Failure modes.** With as few ratings as a chronic-pain REDCap stream typically has, a two-state HMM's dwell-time and misclassification parameters are themselves poorly identified — this needs the same care about effective sample size the codebase already applies to autocorrelated series (`stats_utils.py: effective_n`, `block_length_for`). If the HMM's "flare" state ends up defined mostly by a handful of extreme scores, its smoothing can manufacture structure rather than reveal it; the state assignments should be shown to the clinician, not hidden inside a black-box correlation.

**Exists in code today?** No hidden Markov or state-space model of the pain-rating series exists anywhere in the repository. `stats_utils.py` and `analytics.py` treat every matched rating as a fixed value. Reached by no page.

### 2.3 Aperiodic/periodic spectral decomposition (FOOOF/specparam) — kept as an explanatory report only, never deployed

**The idea.** A power spectrum is a mix of a smooth, broadband "1/f" background (the aperiodic part) and, on top of it, one or more oscillatory bumps (the periodic part). specparam (formerly FOOOF) fits both parts separately, so a change in a band's power can be attributed to a shift in the whole background versus a genuine change in one oscillation's height or width. This has become a standard tool in Parkinson's adaptive-DBS research: the aperiodic exponent (how steep the background slope is) has been proposed as a marker of the brain's excitation/inhibition balance and shows good-to-excellent test-retest consistency for beta-peak power, though only moderate consistency for the aperiodic offset and exponent themselves, in long-term subthalamic recordings (2025 npj Parkinson's Disease study on long-term consistency of aperiodic and periodic physiomarkers [4]). For pain specifically, however, a large multiverse re-analysis of EEG aperiodic components found no reliable relationship to chronic pain across many reasonable analysis choices (2024-25 eLife registered report on excitation/inhibition balance in chronic pain [5]) — a caution this project should weigh before investing here.

**Why this cannot be deployed, restated plainly.** The Percept RC's control law sums the squared spectrum magnitude inside one fixed 5 Hz window and compares that single number to a threshold (`DEVICE_percept_rc.md` §2, §7). It has no way to compute a slope, an offset, or "how much of this band's power is oscillatory versus broadband." This project already built an aperiodic-fit model for exactly this reason and then deleted it: decision 206 records that the fit "reached no page" and that "the device cannot threshold a peak's prominence." That reasoning is unchanged by anything in the literature search above; if anything, the negative multiverse result for pain specifically makes rebuilding it a lower priority than it was in 2026-06.

**How it could still be useful, narrowly.** As a one-time, offline explanatory report — not a recurring computation, not a stored kind, not anything a page reads — specparam could tell a clinician *why* the grid's currently-best band correlates: is it because a genuine oscillatory peak near that frequency tracks pain, or because the whole spectrum's background level shifts with pain (in which case almost any nearby band would show a similar, spurious-looking correlation, and the choice of exact centre frequency is closer to arbitrary than the grid's presentation suggests)? That is a caveat worth writing next to a chosen band, not a new deployable feature.

**Using RCS08's data and the statistic reported.** Run specparam once on the montage-survey and on-demand-streaming spectra already available (the same tiles the grid reads), separately for low-pain and high-pain matched windows, and report: the aperiodic exponent and offset in each state, and whether the grid's winning band sits on a genuine spectral peak or on the flank of the broadband shift. The clinician reads a single sentence: "the [committed band]'s pain correlation looks like [a genuine oscillatory peak / mostly a broadband shift that several neighbouring bands would also show]."

**Failure modes.** specparam's peak-detection settings (number of peaks, minimum peak height) can manufacture or erase a "peak" depending on tuning: any report using it must show the fit and its residual, not just the extracted numbers. And the device's own power reading is thin on RCS08, in a way that reads two ways depending on what is counted (corrected 2026-09-23, panel B item 6): counted over every continuous 3-second window of the record it covers well under 2% (`DEVICE_percept_rc.md` §8, "How thin the native coverage is": on R 0⁻3⁺, 797 device windows against 62,784 voltage-trace tiles), yet counted over the MATCHED PAIN-REPORT rows it answers 79% of them on that same contact (decision 106: 358 of 451 reports, because a report with no voltage trace in its window falls back to the device's own snapshot). Different denominators, not a contradiction. Either way, almost every specparam fit on the continuous record would run on the PSD computed from the voltage trace, which is a different quantity from what the device itself thresholds — the explanatory value stays at the "did this look like a real oscillation" level, not at the level of the deployed number.

**Exists in code today?** A structurally similar aperiodic (1/f) fit existed in this codebase and was deleted (decision 206, module `psd_lsb_model.py` and its `fooof` transform, removed 2026-09-19 per decision digest). Rebuilding it as a non-deployed explanatory report is a distinct, smaller proposal than what was deleted, but shares its central limitation.

### 2.4 Change-point detection on the chronic 10-minute power log, anchored to documented therapy changes

**The idea.** Rather than correlate same-day band power against same-day pain (which cannot separate "the band moved because the setting changed" from "the band moved because the day was different for some unrelated reason"), look for a detectable shift — a change point — in the chronic record's level or variance at the moment a reprogramming is logged, and nowhere else. Bayesian online change-point detection has been used for exactly this kind of problem in other physiological monitoring settings: predicting clinical-state transitions from continuous physiological streams without needing to know in advance how many states exist (an early example is apnea/bradycardia transition detection in preterm infants, and more recent variants pair change-point detection with dynamic time warping for stress-state detection from wearables) [11].

**The model.** A change-point detector (offline, since this is retrospective analysis, not real-time monitoring) run on the chronic band-power series for a committed candidate band, with the *known* times of programmed therapy changes (from `GroupHistory`/`TherapySnapshot`, already decoded) marked as candidate change points to test, rather than searched for blindly across the whole record. The statistic reported is: did a change point fall within some short window of the documented reprogramming date, and how large was the level shift compared with the series' own baseline variability (using the same MAD-based outlier rule already standardized at `stats_utils.py: mad_outlier_flags`, so this analysis does not invent a second outlier convention).

**Using RCS08's data.** The chronic record (`DiagnosticData.LFPTrendLogs`) is already pulled into the store as the raw `acquisition_timeline` kind (decision 216) and into `lsb_series`'s native tier (`availability.py`). RCS08's device sensing frequency has itself been reassigned several times (documented in `DEVICE_percept_rc.md` §10 — the 8.8 Hz contact was moved off that frequency on 2025-12-05 and settled by 2026-02-15), which is exactly the kind of event a change-point analysis anchored to *known* configuration changes should test rather than a blind search stumbling on it and mistaking a sensing reassignment for a physiological effect.

**How a clinician reads it.** "After the [date] reprogramming, this band's level shifted by [X] device units, more than [Y] times its own day-to-day scatter, within [Z] hours of the documented change" — or, if nothing is found: "no detectable shift near any documented reprogramming; whatever this band tracks, it does not obviously respond to the settings changed so far."

**Failure modes.** The chronic record averages over 10 minutes and both a therapy change and a hundred other things (sleep, activity, mood) happen on any given day, so a level shift near a reprogramming date is suggestive, not proof of causation — this is a weaker claim than a within-visit titration ladder, and the report must say so. This method also cannot be built into a drift-over-months term (which decision 196 rules out); it is scoped to "a level shift at a known, dated event," which is a different and much narrower claim than "power drifts with time since disease onset."

**Exists in code today?** No change-point routine exists in the Biomarkers or ClosedLoopDeployment modules today. The closest existing machinery is the state-space local-level and two-component Kalman fit in `ClosedLoopDeployment/design_rule.py` (`fit_design_model`), built for the unrelated purpose of placing an adaptive-threshold switching value robustly against the band's own noise (the contest-winning design rule, decision 150) — it is a level-tracking filter, not a change-point detector, and it is not read by the Biomarkers grid. Reached by no page for this purpose.

### 2.5 EMA recall-bias correction applied to the label before matching

**The idea.** A REDCap or clinic-sheet pain rating is not a neutral sample of "how much pain right now" — a substantial body of ecological-momentary-assessment research shows people's *recalled* pain over a day or week is systematically pulled toward the peak and the most recent (end) values they experienced, and that people who report *more variable* momentary pain also show larger peak/end recall bias (Stone, Schwartz, Broderick and Shiffman, 2005, *Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin* [9]; Schneider, Stone, Schwartz and Broderick on peak/end effects within a single day's recall [10]). None of this is currently modelled: `pain_series` in `availability.py` and the matching layer in `DecodeCommon/matching.py` treat a rating as a single unbiased number at a single timestamp.

**The model.** Where multiple ratings exist close together in time (for example, several clinic-sheet entries across one visit day), estimate a peak/end-weighted recall correction and compare it against the raw average, using the same kind of per-participant calibration this project already applies elsewhere (it is used to a whole conversion constant, so the appetite for "calibrate rather than assume" already exists here). Where only single daily ratings exist (RCS08's typical REDCap cadence), the more modest, defensible step is simply to flag ratings taken shortly after activity or a symptom spike as potentially peak-weighted, rather than silently averaging them in as equivalent to a rating taken during a calm baseline period.

**Using RCS08's data and the statistic reported.** This is a label-only preprocessing step; it changes what number the grid correlates against, not how the grid computes. The report would show, band by band, how much the correlation moves when ratings are adjusted for a simple recency/peak weighting versus taken at face value — analogous to the two-heat-map comparison described for the hidden Markov model above (§2.2), and in fact the two ideas are complementary rather than competing (one models discrete states, the other models a continuous recall bias).

**Failure modes.** Chronic-pain patients rating once a day do not generate the dense same-day repeated measures this literature was built on (most of the underlying studies use multiple prompts per day); applying a peak/end correction to a single daily rating requires either richer within-day sampling than RCS08 currently has, or borrowing a population-average bias correction that may not fit this one participant well — exactly the kind of assumption the house rules ask to be stated rather than hidden.

**Exists in code today?** No recall-bias correction exists anywhere in the repository. Reached by no page.

## 3. Symptom time-series tracking methods (separate treatment)

These methods characterize or denoise the pain-rating series itself, independent of any neural signal. They matter here because every proposal above that touches the label (§2.2, §2.5) depends on picking one of these first.

| Method | What it does to the rating series | Sparse-rating fit | Cost | Decision touched |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Bayesian beta-binomial / zero-one-inflated mixed model** | models a bounded pain score (0-10 or 0-100) honestly, including the common pile-up of scores at the extremes, with per-participant random effects on both the mean and the variance | built specifically for EMA pain data with exactly this shape (a 2026 methods paper models "frequent minimum and maximum scores" in patient-reported pain trajectories [12]; a related beta-binomial mixed-effects model targets the same zero/one-inflation [13]) | medium — needs a Bayesian fitting library not currently in this stack (statsmodels and rpy2/pymer4 are used elsewhere per `CLAUDE.md` §3, but no Bayesian MCMC tool is listed) | none directly; changes only how "how many independent ratings do we have" is counted, which should sharpen, not loosen, decision 184's coverage rule |
| **Two/three-state hidden Markov model with misclassification** (detailed in §2.2) | infers a small number of latent symptom states and how long each typically lasts, treating each raw rating as an imperfect read of the state | designed for irregular, infrequent, possibly-mislabelled longitudinal outcomes (Bureau 2003 [7]; Benoit 2016 [8]) | medium | none directly |
| **Linear hierarchical location-scale model** | separately models each person's mean pain level *and* their day-to-day variability, letting variability itself be a tracked quantity (already used on a 140-person chronic-low-back-pain EMA cohort [6]) | works at n=1 as the participant-level output of a model that would otherwise need a cohort; on a single participant it degenerates to modelling the mean and variance of that one person's series over time windows, which is still informative (is variability itself trending?) | medium | interacts with "time is modelled nowhere" (193-196) only if variability is modelled as a function of calendar time; framing it as "variability within each visit window" avoids that |
| **EMA recall-bias correction** (detailed in §2.5) | adjusts a recalled/summary rating toward what continuous momentary sampling would likely have shown | needs either dense same-day sampling or an accepted population correction | low-medium | none |
| **Change-point detection on the rating series alone** (distinct from §2.4's signal-side version) | finds a date where the *rating* series' level shifted, independent of any signal | works with as few as a handful of ratings per regime, though confidence scales with count | low — same detector as §2.4, applied to the label instead of the neural signal | none, provided it is reported as "a shift on this date," not "pain drifts with time since implant" |
| **Interrupted time series on the rating series around a reprogramming** | tests whether the *reported pain*, not just the band power, shifted after a documented change | Yes, this is the standard use case for interrupted time series in single-subject designs [14] | low-medium — same statistical machinery as §1's chronic-signal ITS row, applied to ratings | complements, not reverses, decision 196 if scoped to one dated event |
| **N-of-1 trial framing of the ladder visits** | treats each visit's amplitude ladder as a mini-trial and asks for a defensible individual causal effect estimate, following the methodological literature built specifically for single-patient designs in chronic pain (Kravitz's NIH-funded "N-of-1 Trials Using mHealth in Chronic Pain" program [15]; a 2026 review of n-of-1 methodological foundations [16]) | built for exactly this — single patient, few, high-quality repeated observations | high — statistically demanding, and this project's own methods document already flags that the ladders are not independently replicated across days for several rates (`METHODS_measurement_and_findings.md` §5: "there is no cross-day replication at 55 Hz at all") | **out of scope for this report** — overlaps with the E1/titration causal-effect work assigned to the Bayesian-optimization / titration worker; noted here only because it is a symptom-tracking-adjacent design, not pursued further |

## 4. Hybrid designs that keep the device constraint

Three designs, each explicit about what runs offline (allowed to be arbitrarily rich) and what the device actually receives (must stay a single band, a threshold or two, and the timing parameters in `DecodeCommon/device_ranges.py`).

**Hybrid 1 — multivariate decoder for selection, univariate grid for deployment.** Run the §2.1 multivariate decoder across all bands and both leads purely to rank candidate bands by how much of the joint model's accuracy each one alone recovers. Feed only the top-ranked band(s) into the existing grid pipeline (`analytics.py`) for the one-band rule check (falls with current, rises with pain; decisions 199, 210) and the sensing-pair flanking check (decision 217). The device never sees anything but the single band and its threshold, exactly as today; the multivariate model's only artifact is a ranked list and its AUC-gap diagnostic (§2.1), stored (if at all) as a non-deployed exploratory report rather than a new store kind that any page reads by default. This directly answers a question the current grid cannot: is the grid's single winning band actually competitive, or is it winning only because nothing better was tried?

**Hybrid 2 — latent-state label smoothing feeding the unchanged Pearson-correlation grid.** Fit the §2.2 hidden Markov model (or the §2.5 recall-bias correction, or both) on the rating series alone, entirely independent of any neural signal. Substitute the smoothed/corrected label for the raw rating as the grid's y-axis, and re-run the identical statistical pipeline already in production — same block bootstrap, same shuffle p, same Benjamini-Hochberg correction (`stats_utils.py`). Nothing about the grid's deployable output (a single band, a Pearson r, an "established"/"supported" verdict) changes shape; only the input rating series is denoised first. This is the lowest-risk hybrid: it touches no signal-side code, no device-facing schema, and no standing decision about the signal (only decision 184's rating-coverage bookkeeping, which it should tighten).

**Hybrid 3 — within-visit change-point/curvature detector plus the chronic-record grid, reconciled rather than merged.** Keep the existing chronic 22-band correlation grid exactly as it is (the "which band tracks pain across the whole record" question). Separately, run the §2.4 change-point detector and the existing amplitude-response curvature test (decisions 55, 56, 124, already implemented) on each visit's titration ladder (the "does this band actually move because of stimulation, within one visit" question). Report the two answers side by side for each candidate band, explicitly not combined into one number: a band that is "established" on the chronic grid but shows no detectable within-visit response to stimulation is a different, weaker candidate than one that is both established and moves under a documented ladder — the device's own "unresolved control problem" (`DEVICE_percept_rc.md` §2, a band that rises then falls with current defeats the two-point threshold logic) makes this reconciliation more than an academic nicety: a band that correlates with pain but does not respond in a controllable, monotonic way to the very variable (current) the device manipulates is not deployable regardless of how strong its correlation looks.

All three hybrids share one property worth stating once, plainly: **the offline model is always richer than the device, and the device only ever receives what it can already compute today** — a single band's power, averaged over a device-legal window, compared to one or two switching values (`DEVICE_percept_rc.md` §2; `DecodeCommon/device_ranges.py`). None of the three requires a code path that writes to the device; none exists in this platform and none is proposed here (`DEVICE_percept_rc.md` §1: "there is no interface that writes a setting to the device").

## 5. Recommendations, ranked

1. **Build Hybrid 2 first (latent-state label smoothing).** It is the cheapest of the four proposals to build (no new store kind, no signal-side code, reuses the existing grid statistics verbatim), touches no standing decision about the signal, and directly answers the question decision 229 leaves open — the exploratory search found that with clinic sheets included, 11 to 22 of 22 bands move together with pain, which reads more like a global, noisy-label effect than a specific band effect. A cleaner label is the natural next diagnostic before spending more effort on band selection.
2. **Build the §2.1 multivariate-decoder-for-selection next (Hybrid 1).** It answers a question this project has not yet asked of its own data: given everything available (both leads, every band, every window length), is the current one-band grid's winner actually close to the best a much richer model could do? A large gap would be an important, sobering finding for a clinician about to be handed a single number; a small gap is reassuring and cheap to state.
3. **Build the §2.4 change-point analysis of the chronic record (as part of Hybrid 3), scoped strictly to documented events.** It uses the one recording stream the device itself actually adapts on (the chronic 10-minute log, per `DEVICE_percept_rc.md` §3-4) rather than only in-clinic streaming, and it is a natural companion to the existing amplitude-response curvature work rather than a replacement for it.
4. **Treat the aperiodic/periodic decomposition (§2.3) as a one-off explanatory report only, if built at all, and read the negative pain-specific multiverse finding [5] as a reason to keep expectations modest.** This project already tried something similar and removed it (decision 206) for a reason — the device cannot threshold a spectral shape — and that reason has not changed. If it is rebuilt, it must be clearly labelled as never feeding a deployable number.
5. **Do not build the coherence-between-leads or the DTW/event-alignment proposals as anything beyond a diagnostic footnote**, at least until the higher-value items above are built. Coherence cannot itself become a deployable signal (the device senses one contact pair), and DTW's benefit depends on having many more ladder steps per band than RCS08's record currently offers.
6. **Explicitly decline, in this report, to pursue the Kalman/particle latent-pain-state filter (from §1's table) as a near-term build.** It is the most literature-supported idea for combining sparse ratings with continuous neural features, but RCS08's own device-native power coverage is under 2 percent of the record (`DEVICE_percept_rc.md` §6), so fitting a joint neural-plus-rating state-space model here would run mostly on modelled, not measured, power — exactly the situation the device documentation already flags as the weakest link in this record. Revisit if native coverage grows.

## 6. Gaps and what I could not verify

- **I did not confirm RCS08's exact current rating counts, ladder-day counts, or per-band native-coverage numbers by running any code.** Every quantitative fact about RCS08 used above (rating cadence, 131 paired calibration blocks, under-2-percent native coverage, "no cross-day replication at 55 Hz", decision 229's 252-setting exploratory search results) is quoted from the project's own documents (`DEVICE_percept_rc.md`, `METHODS_measurement_and_findings.md`, `DECISIONS_and_open_items.md`) rather than re-measured live, per this project's own rule that a document's numbers must be re-run rather than trusted (`CLAUDE.md` §8 rule 3). A build of any proposal above must re-derive its own numbers rather than cite this report's restatements.
- **I did not read the full 382 KB of `analytics.py`** (it exceeds the file-size limit for a single read); I read the sections most relevant to the grid, the calibration constants, and the ceiling/outlier rules, and grepped for the "established"/"supported" verdict logic. There may be additional relevant machinery (for example, an existing partial-correlation or covariate-adjustment helper) that a fuller read would surface; `stats_utils.py: partial_corr` exists and is at least a partial answer to "adjust for stimulation amplitude before correlating," which none of the five proposals above explicitly incorporated — a build step should check whether `partial_corr` already covers part of what a hybrid design would otherwise build fresh.
- **The literature search relied on secondary summaries (search-engine synthesis) rather than full-text reads of every primary paper.** Confidence on the specific numeric claims quoted from Gilron 2021, Oehrn 2024, Provenza 2021 and Scangos 2021 is medium: the existence and general approach of each study is well corroborated across multiple independent search results, but exact figures (sample sizes, effect sizes) were not independently re-verified against the PDF text.
- **I could not find a published account of any group deploying a pain-specific adaptive band on a Percept RC specifically** (as opposed to Parkinson's beta-band or the Summit RC+S trial this project already documents in `DEVICE_percept_rc.md` §13). The comparison table's "how other aDBS groups picked a single band" evidence (Gilron, Oehrn, Provenza, Scangos) is drawn entirely from Parkinson's, OCD and depression closed-loop work on different devices (Summit RC+S, an implantable recording-capable system used investigationally) — the device-constraint reasoning transfers, but no pain-Percept precedent exists to cite directly.
- **I did not verify whether any Bayesian/MCMC fitting library is actually available in the container or host Python environments** beyond what `CLAUDE.md` §3 lists (statsmodels; mixed models through rpy2 and pymer4). If a hierarchical Bayesian model (§3's beta-binomial row) is built, the first step should be confirming what is installed, since none of the packages named in `CLAUDE.md` is a general-purpose MCMC/probabilistic-programming tool.
- **Whether the EMA recall-bias literature's dense same-day sampling assumption applies to RCS08 specifically** was not checked against the actual rating timestamps; if RCS08's REDCap cadence is roughly one rating per day, the recall-bias correction in §2.5 may have too little same-day structure to calibrate against and would need to fall back to a population-average correction, which is a materially weaker claim.
